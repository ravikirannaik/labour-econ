# Internal Migration in India

India is a country of migrants. Yet for decades, internal migration remained statistically invisible, politically neglected, and academically understudied relative to its enormous scale. The textbook model of labour mobility -- developed primarily from the American experience of frictionless interstate movement -- breaks down spectacularly in a country where language, caste, welfare entitlements, and housing markets conspire to keep workers far less mobile than income differentials would predict. For students of labour economics, Indian internal migration is not merely an application of Labor Mobility theory -- it is a stress test of the standard model, revealing both its power and its limits.

---

## Scale and Patterns

### The Numbers

The most comprehensive count of internal migrants in India comes from the Census, which records **place of last residence** and **place of birth**. The 2011 Census enumerated approximately **450 million lifetime migrants** -- persons residing in a place different from their place of birth. This makes India home to the largest internal migration flow in the world by absolute numbers.

!!! indian "Key Migration Statistics"
    | Indicator | Estimate | Source |
    |-----------|----------|--------|
    | **Lifetime migrants** (different place of birth) | ~450 million | Census 2011 |
    | **Interstate migrants** (last decade) | ~60 million | Economic Survey 2016-17 |
    | **Annual interstate migration flow** | ~9 million | Economic Survey 2016-17 |
    | **Seasonal/circular migrants** | ~100 million (estimated) | Various micro-studies |
    | **Remittances to rural areas** | Significant but poorly measured | NSS, CPHS |

    These numbers almost certainly undercount actual migration. The Census captures only those present at the destination on enumeration night. Short-term, seasonal, and circular migrants -- who shuttle between origin and destination over the course of a year -- are systematically missed.

### Composition

The dominant stream is **rural-to-urban migration**, driven by the persistent wage gap between agricultural and non-agricultural employment. However, the composition is more varied than textbook treatments suggest:

- **Marriage migration** is the single largest category in Census data, accounting for roughly half of all female migrants. This is primarily rural-to-rural movement associated with patrilocal marriage norms and is economically distinct from labour migration.
- **Labour migration** dominates male interstate flows. The Economic Survey 2016-17, drawing on railway passenger data and other proxies, estimated that roughly 9 million people moved across state borders annually for work during 2011-2016.
- **Rural-to-rural** migration for agricultural labour -- following the harvest calendar from state to state -- is substantial but almost invisible in official statistics.

## Key Corridors

Internal migration in India follows well-established corridors that reflect the geography of economic opportunity.

!!! indian "Major Migration Corridors"
    **Origin states (labour surplus):** Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha, Jharkhand, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal, Assam

    **Destination states (labour demand):** Delhi, Maharashtra (Mumbai, Pune), Gujarat (Ahmedabad, Surat), Tamil Nadu (Chennai), Kerala, Karnataka (Bengaluru), Haryana, Punjab

    **Key corridors:**

    - **Bihar/UP to Delhi-NCR:** The largest single corridor. Construction, manufacturing, domestic work, rickshaw-pulling, and street vending absorb millions of workers from the Gangetic plains.
    - **Bihar/UP to Mumbai and Gujarat:** Textile mills in Surat, diamond polishing in Gujarat, and Mumbai's vast service economy draw workers from eastern UP and Bihar.
    - **Odisha/Jharkhand to Kerala and Tamil Nadu:** Plantation labour, construction, and fishing industries in southern states recruit heavily from eastern India. The wage differential is particularly stark -- casual daily wages in Kerala can be two to three times those in Odisha.
    - **Northeast to Delhi and Bengaluru:** Young workers from Assam, Manipur, Mizoram, and Nagaland migrate to metropolitan cities, often into the hospitality, retail, and beauty services sectors. They face distinctive challenges including racial discrimination and cultural dislocation.
    - **Rajasthan to Gujarat:** Short-distance seasonal migration for salt pans, cotton harvesting, and brick kilns.

These corridors are remarkably persistent over time, sustained by network effects: once a few pioneers from a village establish themselves at a destination, information and assistance flow back, lowering the migration cost for subsequent movers.

## The Economic Framework

### Migration as Human Capital Investment

The standard economic model of migration, originating with **Sjaastad (1962)**, treats the decision to migrate as an investment in human capital. A worker compares the present value of lifetime earnings at the destination against those at the origin, net of migration costs:

$$
\text{Migrate if } \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{w_d(t) - w_o(t)}{(1+r)^t} > C
$$

where $w_d(t)$ and $w_o(t)$ are destination and origin earnings, $r$ is the discount rate, $T$ is the remaining working life, and $C$ encompasses direct moving costs plus psychic costs of separation from family and community. In the Indian context, $C$ is exceptionally high for reasons discussed below, which explains why migration rates are lower than the wage gap alone would predict.

### The Roy Model and Self-Selection

Who migrates? The **Roy model** (applied to migration by Borjas, 1987) predicts that the selection pattern depends on the return to skill in origin and destination. If the destination rewards skill more than the origin (wider wage distribution), migrants will be **positively selected** -- the most able workers move. If the origin rewards skill more, migrants will be **negatively selected**.

!!! empirical "Self-Selection in Indian Migration"
    The Indian evidence suggests a complex pattern. **Positively selected** migration characterises the high-skill stream: engineers moving from tier-2 cities to Bengaluru, doctors moving to metro hospitals, IIT graduates moving to the US. But the bulk of low-skill interstate migration appears to involve workers from the **lower-middle** of the origin skill distribution -- not the very poorest (who cannot afford to move) nor the well-off (who have adequate local opportunities). This is consistent with the model when migration costs are high: only those whose expected gain exceeds the substantial cost threshold will move, which excludes both tails of the distribution.

    Munshi and Rosenzweig (2016, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*) provide micro-evidence that caste-based insurance networks discourage out-migration of male members, creating a selection pattern driven by social structure rather than purely by individual returns to skill.

## Barriers to Migration

India's internal migration rate is anomalously low given its income differentials. **Kone, Liu, Mattoo, Ozden, and Piracha (2018)** provide the most systematic cross-country comparison, finding that India's interstate migration rate is roughly one-third of what a standard gravity model -- calibrated on cross-country data with similar income gaps, population sizes, and distances -- would predict. Several barriers explain this gap.

### Language Fragmentation

India has 22 scheduled languages and hundreds of dialects. A worker from Bihar (Hindi) moving to Tamil Nadu (Tamil) or Kerala (Malayalam) faces a language barrier with no parallel in the United States or China. Language affects access to information, negotiation of wages, interaction with authorities, and social integration. This is not merely a convenience issue -- it is an economic cost that enters directly into the migration cost term $C$ in the investment model.

### Caste and Kinship Networks

!!! empirical "Caste Networks as Migration Barriers"
    **Munshi and Rosenzweig (2016, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*)** show that caste-based mutual insurance networks -- which provide informal social protection against income shocks in the origin village -- actively discourage spatial mobility. Male members of well-functioning jati networks are less likely to migrate, because migration would sever their access to the network's insurance function and reduce the network's risk-pooling capacity. The key insight is that caste networks are **geographically bounded**: they provide insurance only in the local area. A worker who migrates loses this safety net without gaining a formal substitute at the destination. This creates a migration penalty that is invisible in standard economic models but quantitatively large.

### Non-Portability of Welfare Entitlements

India's social protection architecture is deeply place-based. The **Public Distribution System** (PDS) -- which provides subsidised food grain to roughly 800 million people -- is tied to ration cards issued at the place of residence. A migrant worker from Bihar working on a construction site in Gujarat could not, until recently, use their ration card in Gujarat. Similarly, access to public housing, school admissions, and health services is typically tied to domicile status.

!!! policy "The Portability Problem"
    The non-portability of entitlements acts as an implicit tax on migration. A worker who moves loses access to subsidised food, children's school places, and potential MGNREGA employment at home. The Aadhaar-based identity system was expected to solve this by enabling portable, individual-level entitlements. In practice, Aadhaar has facilitated financial inclusion but has not fully solved the portability problem -- PDS portability through the **One Nation One Ration Card** (ONORC) scheme was announced in 2019 but remained incompletely implemented when the COVID-19 crisis exposed its gaps.

### Housing and Informal Settlement

Migrant workers in Indian cities overwhelmingly live in informal housing -- slum clusters, construction-site dormitories, shared rooms with shift-sleeping arrangements, or pavement dwellings. Formal rental housing markets are thin and exclusionary (landlords discriminate by religion, caste, region of origin, and dietary habits). The absence of affordable housing at the destination raises $C$ substantially and helps explain why so much Indian migration is temporary and circular rather than permanent.

### Sons-of-the-Soil Politics

Political movements targeting migrants have recurred across Indian states. The Shiv Sena in Maharashtra (targeting South Indians in the 1960s, then North Indians from the 1990s onward), demands for domicile-based job quotas in Jharkhand, Assam, and Haryana, and periodic violence against migrant workers in the Northeast all raise the perceived risk and psychic cost of migration. These political barriers have no close analogue in the US textbook model.

## Seasonal and Circular Migration

A distinctive feature of Indian internal migration is the prevalence of **seasonal and circular** movement -- workers who migrate for part of the year and return to their home villages for the rest. This pattern is driven by the agricultural calendar, the seasonal nature of demand in key industries, and the desire to maintain village-based entitlements and social ties.

!!! indian "Key Sectors Using Seasonal Migrant Labour"
    - **Construction:** India's largest employer of seasonal migrants. Workers from Bihar, Odisha, Jharkhand, and Chhattisgarh staff construction sites across urban India. They are recruited through labour contractors (*thekedars*) who advance wages, arrange transport, and often exercise considerable control over working conditions.
    - **Brick kilns:** An estimated 10-15 million workers, many from Odisha and Chhattisgarh, spend 6-8 months per year moulding bricks in kilns across western and southern India. Families migrate together, and children frequently work alongside parents. Piece-rate payment is universal.
    - **Sugarcane harvesting:** In Maharashtra, roughly 1 million workers -- predominantly from the Marathwada drought belt -- migrate annually for the October-March sugarcane cutting season. They are recruited by *mukadams* (contractors) who advance loans before the season, creating debt-bondage dynamics.
    - **Salt pans:** Workers from Rajasthan and Kutch spend months at coastal salt pans under extreme conditions.
    - **Domestic work:** Increasingly, young women from Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and tribal areas of West Bengal migrate to Delhi and other metros as domestic workers, often through placement agencies with limited accountability.

**Deshingkar and Akter (2009)** provide the most comprehensive overview of seasonal migration patterns, documenting how the contractor system -- rather than individual decision-making -- mediates much of this movement. The textbook model of the atomistic individual comparing wage differentials understates the role of intermediaries who recruit, transport, and supervise migrant labour in exchange for a share of their wages.

Women's participation in seasonal migration is substantial but varies by sector. In brick kilns and sugarcane harvesting, entire families migrate. In construction, male-dominated crews are more common, though women increasingly participate in urban construction. In domestic work, the flow is overwhelmingly female.

## MGNREGA and Migration

!!! empirical "NREGA Reduces Seasonal Out-Migration"
    **Imbert and Papp (2015, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*)** provide rigorous evidence that MGNREGA reduced seasonal out-migration from rural areas. Using the programme's phased rollout across districts as a natural experiment, they find that NREGA districts experienced approximately a **10 per cent decline in temporary out-migration** during the agricultural lean season. The mechanism is the reservation wage channel: by providing guaranteed employment at the NREGA wage in the home village, the programme raised the opportunity cost of migrating. Workers who would otherwise have endured the costs of seasonal migration to urban construction sites or brick kilns chose instead to work on NREGA projects locally.

    This is a direct application of the migration investment model: NREGA raised $w_o(t)$ (the origin wage), reducing the net gain from migration below the cost threshold $C$ for marginal migrants. See the [NREGA essay](nrega.md) for a detailed treatment of the programme's labour market effects.

The welfare implications of reduced migration are ambiguous. If NREGA enables workers to avoid exploitative seasonal work under harsh conditions, the welfare gain is clear. But if it discourages migration that would yield higher lifetime earnings, the long-run human capital consequences are less favourable. The evidence suggests that NREGA primarily reduces **distress migration** -- movement undertaken not as a productive investment but as a survival strategy during the lean season -- which is welfare-improving on net.

## The COVID-19 Migrant Crisis

The nationwide lockdown announced on 24 March 2020, with four hours' notice, exposed the invisibility of India's migrant workforce with devastating clarity.

### The Reverse Migration Wave

With factories, construction sites, and markets shuttered overnight, an estimated **10-12 million migrant workers** attempted to return to their home villages. Lacking transport (railways and inter-state buses were suspended), savings (most had fewer than two weeks' worth), and housing (dormitories were closed), thousands began **walking hundreds of kilometres** along national highways. Images of families with children walking on expressways became the defining visual of India's pandemic experience.

!!! policy "What the Crisis Revealed"
    The migrant crisis exposed multiple, compounding policy failures:

    - **No data:** The government did not know how many migrant workers were in which cities. There was no registry, no database, no count. The Census (last conducted in 2011) was a decade old. The PLFS does not track short-term migrants effectively.
    - **No portability:** Migrants could not access PDS rations at their work location. The ONORC scheme existed on paper but was not operational in most states.
    - **No housing:** Migrant workers in informal housing had no tenure security. When work stopped, they were evicted or unable to pay rent.
    - **No social protection:** MGNREGA is a rural programme. Urban migrant workers had no equivalent safety net. No unemployment insurance, no emergency cash transfer mechanism reached them promptly.
    - **No coordination:** The response was fragmented across central, state, and local governments, with origin and destination states blaming each other.

    Srivastava (2020) provides an early and comprehensive account of the crisis, documenting the scale of reverse migration, the inadequacy of the policy response, and the structural neglect of migrant workers in Indian social policy.

### Aftermath

The crisis had lasting consequences. Many workers who returned home did not immediately re-migrate, depressing urban labour supply in construction and manufacturing through 2020 and into 2021. MGNREGA demand spiked to record levels as returnees sought rural employment. Some workers reported a permanent change in their migration calculus -- the trauma of the lockdown experience raised the psychic cost $C$ in their migration decision, at least temporarily.

The crisis also forced policy attention. The **e-Shram portal**, launched in 2021, aimed to create a national database of unorganised workers, including migrants, with Aadhaar-linked registration. By 2023, over 290 million workers had registered, though the portal's utility for delivering actual benefits remains limited.

## Policy Landscape

### Legislative Framework

The **Interstate Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1979** was the primary legislation governing interstate labour migration. It required contractors to register migrant workers, provide displacement allowance, and ensure basic amenities. In practice, enforcement was negligible -- the vast majority of migrant workers had never heard of the Act, and labour inspectorates lacked the capacity to monitor compliance.

The 1979 Act was subsumed into the **Code on Social Security, 2020**, one of four labour codes that consolidated 29 central labour laws. The Code includes provisions for a national database of unorganised workers, portability of welfare benefits, and the establishment of a Social Security Fund for gig and platform workers. The labour codes have been passed by Parliament but are yet to be fully implemented, with states required to frame rules for operationalisation.

### Recent Initiatives

!!! policy "Key Policy Responses"
    - **One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC):** Enables PDS beneficiaries to draw their entitled ration from any Fair Price Shop in the country using Aadhaar-linked biometric authentication. Operational in all 36 states and Union Territories as of 2024. A significant step toward entitlement portability, though awareness among migrants remains low.
    - **e-Shram Portal:** A centralised database of unorganised and migrant workers. Provides a Universal Account Number (UAN) linked to Aadhaar. Intended as the infrastructure for future benefit delivery.
    - **PM-SVANidhi (Street Vendor Scheme):** Micro-credit for street vendors, many of whom are migrants, launched during the COVID-19 recovery.
    - **Ayushman Bharat portability:** Health insurance under PM-JAY is technically portable, but operational portability at hospitals across states is uneven.

### What Remains Missing

Despite these initiatives, the fundamental challenge persists: Indian policy is designed around sedentary populations, while the economy depends on mobile labour. A comprehensive migration policy would require portable entitlements across all welfare programmes, enforceable standards for migrant worker housing, urban planning that acknowledges (rather than criminalises) migrant settlement, and a reliable real-time data system that tracks migration flows. India currently has none of these at scale.

# Indian Labour Market Context

Understanding labour economics through the lens of India's diverse and rapidly transforming economy.

---

India's labour market is one of the largest and most complex in the world. With a working-age population exceeding 900 million and a labour force of roughly 560 million (PLFS 2023-24), it dwarfs most advanced economies in scale alone. But scale is only the beginning of the story. What makes the Indian labour market a uniquely instructive case for students of labour economics is the degree to which textbook models must be stretched, qualified, and sometimes rethought to accommodate its distinctive features.

## The Big Picture in Five Facts

**Fact 1: Informality dominates.** Over 90 per cent of Indian workers are employed in the informal sector or hold informal jobs within the formal sector. This means the standard competitive model of a single, unified labour market with full information and flexible wages applies, at best, to a narrow sliver of the economy. The vast majority of workers operate without written contracts, social security coverage, or legal protections.

**Fact 2: Female labour force participation is strikingly low.** India's female LFPR has historically hovered between 20 and 30 per cent -- among the lowest in the world, and well below countries at similar income levels. This is not merely a measurement issue; deep structural, cultural, and demand-side factors suppress women's engagement with paid work in ways that the standard labour supply model must be extended to explain.

**Fact 3: Caste continues to shape labour market outcomes.** Occupational segregation by caste has deep historical roots and persists in modern forms. Wage gaps between Scheduled Castes/Scheduled Tribes and upper-caste workers survive controls for education and experience, pointing to discrimination that the Becker and statistical discrimination models help us analyse.

**Fact 4: Internal migration is massive but poorly measured.** Census 2011 recorded approximately 450 million internal migrants, though many seasonal and circular movements go uncaptured by decennial surveys. The economics of migration -- the Roy model, self-selection, and wage convergence -- finds fertile ground in India's interstate labour flows.

**Fact 5: Policy interventions are ambitious and instructive.** India operates one of the world's largest workfare programmes (NREGA), has experimented with reservation policies for disadvantaged castes, and has recently consolidated 29 labour laws into 4 new labour codes. Each of these offers a natural laboratory for the theories we study in the textbook.

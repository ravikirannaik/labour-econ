# Gender and Labour Force Participation in India

India has one of the lowest female labour force participation rates in the world. This fact is simultaneously well-known and poorly understood. It persists despite rising educational attainment, declining fertility, and rapid economic growth -- all forces that, in the standard labour supply model, should push female LFPR upward. Understanding why Indian women do not participate in paid work at rates comparable to women in other countries at similar income levels requires engaging with measurement problems, social norms, structural transformation, and the specific features of Indian labour demand.

---

## The Headline Numbers

According to the PLFS 2023-24, the female LFPR (UPSS, 15+ years) stood at approximately 41.7 per cent for all-India. This represents a notable increase from the recent trough of roughly 23-24 per cent recorded in 2017-18. The male LFPR, by comparison, has remained stable at around 78-80 per cent.

But the aggregate number masks enormous heterogeneity:

| Dimension | Female LFPR (UPSS, 15+) |
|-----------|:-----------------------:|
| Rural | ~47% |
| Urban | ~28% |
| SC/ST women | Higher than upper-caste women |
| Women with secondary education | Lower than women with no education |

!!! indian "The U-Shaped Relationship with Education"
    One of the most striking patterns in Indian female LFPR is its **U-shape** with respect to education. Women with no education have relatively high participation (driven by economic necessity in agriculture and casual work). Participation drops sharply for women with secondary education -- they have left manual work behind but do not yet have access to white-collar jobs. It rises again for women with graduate and post-graduate degrees who enter professional occupations. This pattern is documented in Klasen and Pieters (2015) and Chatterjee, Murgai, and Rama (2015).

---

## The U-Shaped Hypothesis: Goldin Applied to India

Claudia Goldin (1995, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*) proposed that female LFPR follows a **U-shape** over the course of economic development. In very poor agrarian societies, women participate extensively in farm and household production. As countries industrialise and household incomes rise, an **income effect** pulls women out of the (stigmatised) manufacturing workforce, and social norms restrict women's work outside the home. At higher income levels, the **substitution effect** dominates -- rising education and white-collar job opportunities draw women back into the labour force.

!!! model "The Goldin U-Shape"
    The intuition is straightforward in the textbook framework:

    - At low income levels: the marginal utility of income is very high, so even low wages induce participation. Women work out of necessity.
    - At middle income levels: the household's income (typically the husband's) has risen enough that the income effect dominates. The reservation wage rises above the market wage for available (manual, stigmatised) jobs.
    - At high income levels: women's education has risen, creating a high market wage for professional jobs. The substitution effect dominates, and participation rises.

    Mathematically, the participation condition is:

    \[
    w_{\text{market}} > w_{\text{reservation}}(V, \text{norms}, \text{children}, \text{safety})
    \]

    where the reservation wage is a function of non-labour income \(V\), social norms, childcare responsibilities, and safety concerns. India is arguably stuck at the bottom of the U, where the reservation wage has risen but market wages for women have not risen commensurately.

Several scholars have tested the Goldin hypothesis on Indian data:

- **Chatterjee, Murgai, and Rama (2015)** find evidence consistent with a U-shape across Indian states but argue that India's turning point has not yet arrived, because the types of jobs that draw women back into the labour force (white-collar, service sector) have not expanded fast enough.

- **Klasen and Pieters (2015, *World Development*)** show that the U-shape is driven primarily by the education composition effect: women with intermediate education have very low LFPR, and this group has grown rapidly as a share of the female population, dragging the aggregate rate down.

## Supply-Side Explanations

### Social Norms and Stigma

The most powerful supply-side factor is the role of **social norms** around women's work. In many parts of India, particularly in the Hindi-belt states of northern and central India, women's participation in manual labour outside the home is viewed as a signal of low household status. Families with rising incomes withdraw women from the labour force as a form of **status production** (Deshpande and Kabeer, 2024, *Feminist Economics*).

Field and Jayachandran (2021) show that in settings with strong purdah norms, women's mobility outside the home is severely restricted, directly limiting their access to employment. Bernhardt and co-authors (2018, *AEJ: Applied Economics*) document that in rural Rajasthan, women who took up paid work experienced lower life satisfaction, suggesting that social sanctions against women's work are real and costly.

### Education

Rising female education has a **paradoxical short-run effect** on LFPR. As girls stay in school longer, they leave the labour force during their educational years (reducing LFPR in the 15-24 age group). And upon completing secondary education, many find that the available jobs do not match their aspirations -- manual agricultural or construction work is "below" their new status, but suitable white-collar or semi-skilled jobs are scarce. This is the "education-aspiration gap."

### Marriage, Fertility, and Household Responsibilities

Marriage is associated with a sharp decline in female LFPR in India. Married women bear a disproportionate burden of domestic work and childcare. The NSO's Time Use Survey (2019) showed that Indian women spend an average of 299 minutes per day on unpaid domestic and care work, compared to 97 minutes for men -- one of the largest gender gaps globally. This unpaid work directly competes with market work for women's limited time, raising the reservation wage.

!!! empirical "The Motherhood Penalty"
    Aggarwal and Sinha (2023) use PLFS data to show that the presence of children under age 5 reduces women's LFPR by approximately 10-12 percentage points, with the effect concentrated among urban women. The effect is larger for women without access to extended family childcare networks. This is the Indian version of the well-documented "motherhood penalty" in the Western literature.

### Safety Concerns

The safety of women in public spaces and workplaces is a binding constraint on female labour supply in India. Borker (2021, *American Economic Review*) demonstrates that women in Delhi are willing to accept significantly longer commutes to avoid routes perceived as unsafe -- effectively shrinking their geographic labour market. Chakraborty and co-authors (2018) find that media reports of sexual violence reduce female employment in the affected areas.

## Demand-Side Explanations

Supply-side factors are not the whole story. The types of jobs available to women matter enormously.

### Structural Transformation without Female Job Creation

India's structural transformation has been unusual: the economy shifted from agriculture to **services** without passing through a labour-intensive manufacturing phase. Countries like Bangladesh, Vietnam, and China absorbed large numbers of women into export-oriented garment and electronics factories. India's manufacturing sector, by contrast, has remained capital-intensive and relatively small, providing few blue-collar jobs for women. The service sector has created professional jobs (IT, healthcare, education) that benefit educated women but leaves out the vast middle.

### Declining Agricultural Employment

As agricultural productivity rises and farm sizes remain tiny, women's employment in agriculture is declining. Between the NSS 2004-05 and PLFS 2017-18, the number of women employed in agriculture fell by an estimated 34 million (Abraham, 2013). Many of these women did not transition to other sectors -- they simply left the labour force.

!!! model "The Missing Middle of Job Creation"
    In the textbook model, wages adjust to clear the market: if women leave agriculture, wages in other sectors should fall to absorb them, or agricultural wages should fall to retain them. In practice, the absence of suitable non-agricultural jobs for women with low-to-moderate education, combined with high reservation wages driven by norms, means that the market does not clear. The result is a large pool of women who are "out of the labour force" -- not unemployed (they are not searching), but not employed either. This is a form of **hidden unemployment** or **disguised withdrawal** that the standard unemployment rate does not capture.

---

## Measurement Issues

Part of the decline in female LFPR observed between 2004-05 and 2017-18 may be a measurement artifact:

- **Undercounting of unpaid family work:** Women who assist on family farms or family enterprises for a few hours a day may not report themselves as "workers" and may not be recorded as such by interviewers. The PLFS UPSS definition captures subsidiary activities (30+ days in the year), but many women's contributions fall below this threshold or are simply not reported.

- **Rising enrolment:** Part of the decline in LFPR among young women (15-24) reflects increased school and college enrolment, which is a positive development.

- **The post-2019 uptick -- real or artifact?** Female LFPR has risen sharply since 2019-20, from roughly 24 per cent to over 40 per cent by 2023-24. Some of this likely reflects genuine increases in women's employment, particularly in agriculture. But some analysts (Kannan and Raveendran, 2019; Abraham, 2023) have raised concerns about whether changes in survey methodology, the shift from July-June to calendar year, and the inclusion of unpaid domestic work adjacent to economic activities may have inflated the numbers.

!!! policy "Is the Post-2019 Rise Real?"
    The debate is not settled. Optimists point to rising self-help group (SHG) participation, expanding gig economy opportunities, and post-COVID recovery as genuine demand-side factors. Sceptics note that much of the increase is concentrated in self-employment and unpaid family work -- categories where the measurement boundary between "economic activity" and "domestic work" is blurriest. As a student, you should be aware of this debate and avoid taking either the optimistic or pessimistic reading at face value.

## Rural vs. Urban Differences

The rural-urban gap in female LFPR is striking and persistent. Rural women participate at nearly double the rate of urban women. This reflects:

1. **Agriculture's role:** Farm work -- own-account cultivation, livestock rearing, and agricultural labour -- absorbs a large share of rural women. These activities are performed close to home and are compatible with childcare.

2. **Urban norm effects:** Urban middle-class norms around women's work are often more restrictive than rural norms. The "income effect" of urbanisation pulls women out of the workforce.

3. **Job availability:** The mismatch between urban women's education and available urban jobs is stark. There are not enough clerical, retail, teaching, or healthcare jobs to absorb the growing number of educated urban women.

## Policy Landscape

Several policies have been designed to raise female LFPR, with mixed results:

**Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act, 2017:** Extended paid maternity leave from 12 weeks to 26 weeks for establishments with 10 or more employees. While beneficial for women who already have formal jobs, critics argue it may discourage firms from hiring women by increasing the expected cost of employing them -- a standard prediction from the labour demand model.

**Mandatory creche provision:** The 2017 amendment also mandated creche facilities in establishments with 50 or more workers. Compliance has been patchy.

**Skill development programmes:** The Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) has a target of training women, but training without corresponding job creation does not solve the demand-side problem.

**Self-Help Groups and NRLM:** The National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) has organised over 90 million women into SHGs, providing microfinance and livelihood support. This has been one of the more successful interventions in raising women's economic participation, though the quality of livelihoods generated remains a concern.

!!! policy "What Would the Textbook Recommend?"
    The standard labour supply model suggests two broad policy levers: (1) **raise the market wage** for women (through skill development, anti-discrimination enforcement, and targeted job creation) to make participation more attractive relative to the reservation wage; (2) **lower the reservation wage** by reducing the cost of non-market time (through childcare provision, flexible work arrangements, and public safety improvements). India needs both simultaneously. The challenge is that social norms operate on a different timescale from policy interventions -- they shift over generations, not election cycles.

# Glossary of Labour Economics Terms

A comprehensive reference of key terms used throughout ECON207, organized alphabetically. Each entry includes the chapter where the term is primarily introduced. Use ++ctrl+f++ (or ++cmd+f++ on Mac) to search.

---

## A

**Ability bias**
:   The upward bias in estimated returns to education that arises because individuals with higher innate ability tend to acquire more schooling *and* earn more, even apart from their education. Omitting ability from a Mincer regression inflates the coefficient on schooling. (Ch. 6)

**Added worker effect**
:   The tendency of secondary household earners (often spouses) to enter the labour force when the primary earner becomes unemployed, in order to maintain household income. Represents a positive labour supply response to a negative income shock. (Ch. 2)

**Adverse selection**
:   A situation in which workers or firms with unfavourable characteristics are disproportionately likely to enter a market or accept a contract. In labour markets, adverse selection can arise in insurance provision, efficiency wage settings, or when firms cannot observe worker quality before hiring. (Ch. 10)

**Affirmative action**
:   Policies that require or encourage employers to take proactive steps to increase representation of historically disadvantaged groups (defined by race, caste, gender, etc.) in hiring, promotion, or contracting. (Ch. 9)

**Audit study**
:   An experimental method for detecting discrimination in which matched pairs of testers (identical in qualifications but differing in a characteristic such as race or gender) apply for jobs or housing, and differential callback or offer rates are measured. (Ch. 9)

**Average product of labour (APL)**
:   Total output divided by the number of workers: $AP_L = q / L$. Useful for comparing productivity levels across firms or over time, but not the relevant margin for hiring decisions. (Ch. 3)

## B

**Backward-bending labour supply curve**
:   A labour supply curve that initially slopes upward (substitution effect dominates) but eventually bends backward at high wages (income effect dominates), so that further wage increases lead to fewer hours supplied. Empirically observed for prime-age males. (Ch. 2)

**Bargaining power**
:   The relative ability of a worker or union to extract surplus from the employment relationship. Depends on outside options, labour market tightness, and institutional factors such as collective bargaining laws. (Ch. 4)

**Between-group inequality**
:   The component of overall wage inequality that is explained by differences in average wages across observable groups (e.g., education levels, experience cohorts, gender). Contrasted with within-group inequality. (Ch. 7)

**Budget constraint**
:   The set of all combinations of consumption and leisure that a worker can afford given their wage rate, non-labour income, and total available time. In the labour supply model, the slope of the budget constraint equals the negative of the wage rate. (Ch. 2)

## C

**Capital-labour ratio**
:   The amount of capital per worker ($K/L$) employed by a firm or in an economy. Changes in the capital-labour ratio affect the marginal product of labour. (Ch. 3)

**Cobweb model**
:   A dynamic model of labour market adjustment in which workers make education and career decisions based on current wages, but the resulting supply response takes time (e.g., years of schooling). The lag between the supply decision and market entry can generate cyclical overshooting and undershooting of wages and employment around equilibrium. (Ch. 4)

**College wage premium**
:   The percentage by which the average earnings of workers with a bachelor's degree exceed those of workers with only a high school diploma. In the US, the premium roughly doubled from about 40% in 1980 to over 80% by the 2010s. (Ch. 6)

**Compensating wage differential**
:   The wage premium (or discount) that a job must offer to attract workers given its non-wage characteristics, such as risk of injury, unpleasant conditions, or irregular hours. Jobs with less desirable attributes must pay more, all else equal. (Ch. 5)

**Competitive equilibrium**
:   The wage and employment level at which the quantity of labour supplied equals the quantity of labour demanded in a competitive labour market. No firm or worker has an incentive to change behaviour at the equilibrium wage. (Ch. 4)

**Complements (in production)**
:   Two inputs are complements if an increase in the quantity of one raises the marginal product of the other. For example, skilled workers and computers are often complements: more computers raise the productivity of skilled workers. (Ch. 3)

**Correspondence study**
:   An experimental method for detecting hiring discrimination in which fictitious resumes that vary only in the characteristic of interest (e.g., name signalling race or caste) are sent to real job postings, and callback rates are compared. (Ch. 9)

**Cross-wage elasticity of labour demand**
:   The percentage change in demand for one type of labour resulting from a one-percent change in the wage of another type. Positive values indicate the inputs are substitutes; negative values indicate complements. (Ch. 3)

**Customer discrimination**
:   Prejudice exercised by customers who prefer to be served by workers of a particular group, leading firms to segregate or pay differential wages to accommodate customer preferences. (Ch. 9)

**Cyclical unemployment**
:   Unemployment that rises during economic recessions and falls during expansions, driven by fluctuations in aggregate demand. Distinguished from frictional and structural unemployment. (Ch. 11)

## D

**Deadweight loss**
:   The loss of total economic surplus (the sum of worker and firm surplus) caused by a market distortion such as a tax, minimum wage, or monopoly. In labour markets, a payroll tax creates a wedge between what firms pay and what workers receive, reducing total employment below the efficient level. (Ch. 4)

**Deferred compensation**
:   A pay structure in which workers are paid below their marginal product early in their career and above it later. This tilted earnings profile reduces turnover, discourages shirking, and rewards loyalty. (Ch. 10)

**Derived demand**
:   The demand for labour (or any factor of production) that arises not because labour is desired for its own sake, but because it is needed to produce goods and services that consumers want. Changes in product demand therefore shift labour demand. (Ch. 3)

**Diminishing marginal returns**
:   The property that, holding other inputs fixed, each successive unit of labour adds less to output than the previous unit. This is the reason the short-run labour demand curve slopes downward. (Ch. 3)

**Discouraged worker effect**
:   The tendency of some workers to stop searching for employment during recessions because they believe no suitable jobs are available. Discouraged workers exit the labour force and are therefore not counted as unemployed, which can cause the measured unemployment rate to understate true joblessness. (Ch. 2)

**Discrimination coefficient**
:   In Becker's model of taste-based discrimination, the utility cost ($d$) that a prejudiced employer, customer, or co-worker associates with transacting with members of a particular group. The employer behaves as if the cost of hiring a minority worker is $w(1 + d)$ rather than $w$. (Ch. 9)

## E

**Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)**
:   A refundable tax credit in the United States targeted at low-income working families. It provides a wage subsidy that phases in with earnings, plateaus, and then phases out. The EITC creates positive labour force participation incentives (extensive margin) but may reduce hours for those in the phase-out range (intensive margin). (Ch. 2)

**Efficiency wage**
:   A wage set above the market-clearing level by a profit-maximizing firm in order to reduce shirking, lower turnover, attract higher-quality applicants, or improve morale. Because all firms may set efficiency wages, the resulting aggregate excess supply of labour manifests as equilibrium unemployment. (Ch. 10)

**Elasticity of labour demand**
:   The percentage change in employment resulting from a one-percent change in the wage: $\eta = (\%\Delta L) / (\%\Delta w)$. Hamermesh's (1993) consensus estimate places the own-wage elasticity at roughly $-0.30$. (Ch. 3)

**Elasticity of labour supply**
:   The percentage change in hours worked (or labour force participation) resulting from a one-percent change in the wage. Empirical estimates are small and positive for men, larger and positive for women, and vary by margin (extensive vs. intensive). (Ch. 2)

**Employer discrimination**
:   Discrimination by employers who are willing to pay a premium to avoid hiring workers from a particular group, acting as if minority workers cost more than their actual wage. (Ch. 9)

**Employment-population ratio**
:   The fraction of the working-age population that is currently employed. Unlike the LFPR, it is not affected by changes in the number of discouraged workers leaving the labour force. (Ch. 1)

## F

**Firm-specific training**
:   See *Specific training*. (Ch. 6)

**Frictional unemployment**
:   Short-duration unemployment that arises because it takes time for workers and firms to find suitable matches, even in a healthy labour market. It is a normal feature of a dynamic economy with job creation and destruction. (Ch. 11)

## G

**General training**
:   Training that increases a worker's productivity equally at the current firm and at all other firms. Under competition, workers bear the cost of general training (through lower wages during training) and capture the returns, because any firm would be willing to pay the higher post-training wage. (Ch. 6)

**Gini coefficient**
:   A summary measure of inequality ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). Geometrically, it equals the area between the Lorenz curve and the 45-degree line of perfect equality, divided by the total area under the 45-degree line. (Ch. 7)

**Gross complements**
:   Two inputs whose demand moves in the same direction: when the price of input $j$ rises, demand for input $i$ falls. The scale effect dominates the substitution effect. (Ch. 3)

**Gross substitutes**
:   Two inputs whose demand moves in opposite directions: when the price of input $j$ rises, demand for input $i$ rises. The substitution effect dominates the scale effect. (Ch. 3)

## H

**Hedonic wage function**
:   The equilibrium relationship between wages and job characteristics (e.g., risk of injury, amenities) that emerges from the matching of heterogeneous workers and firms. The slope of the hedonic wage function with respect to a particular attribute measures the market's implicit price of that attribute. (Ch. 5)

**Human capital**
:   The stock of skills, knowledge, and experience that makes a worker productive. Investments in human capital include formal education, on-the-job training, and health care. The concept was formalized by Becker (1964) and Mincer (1974). (Ch. 6)

## I

**Immigration surplus**
:   The net gain in national income accruing to natives as a result of immigration. It arises because immigrants increase the supply of labour, which lowers wages and raises the return to capital and complementary native labour. The surplus is proportional to the square of the immigrant share and the elasticity of labour demand. (Ch. 8)

**Income effect**
:   In the labour supply model, the change in hours of work caused by a change in income holding the wage rate constant. An increase in income (e.g., through a lottery win or inheritance) makes the worker "richer" and leads to an increase in the consumption of all normal goods, including leisure -- thus reducing labour supply. (Ch. 2)

**Indifference curve**
:   A curve showing all combinations of consumption ($C$) and leisure ($\ell$) that yield the same level of utility to a worker. Higher indifference curves represent greater well-being. The slope of the indifference curve is the marginal rate of substitution between consumption and leisure. (Ch. 2)

**Informal employment**
:   Employment that is not covered by formal labour laws, social protection, or written contracts. In India, over 80% of employment is informal. Informal workers typically lack job security, benefits, and recourse to legal protections. (Ch. 1)

**Isocost line**
:   A line showing all combinations of labour and capital that a firm can purchase for a given total cost: $C = wL + rK$. The slope of the isocost line is $-w/r$, the relative price of labour to capital. (Ch. 3)

**Isoprofit curve**
:   In the compensating differentials model, a curve showing all combinations of wages and job amenities (e.g., safety levels) that yield the same level of profit to a firm. Higher isoprofit curves represent lower profit. Firms with lower costs of providing safety have flatter isoprofit curves. (Ch. 5)

**Isoquant**
:   A curve showing all combinations of labour and capital that produce the same level of output. Analogous to an indifference curve in consumer theory. The slope of the isoquant is the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS). (Ch. 3)

## J

**Job finding rate**
:   The probability that an unemployed worker finds a job within a given period (typically one month). Together with the separation rate, it determines the steady-state unemployment rate. (Ch. 11)

**Job matching**
:   The process by which workers and firms form employment relationships based on the quality of the match between worker skills and job requirements. Good matches produce higher output and are more durable; poor matches dissolve quickly. (Ch. 8)

## K

**Keynesian unemployment**
:   Unemployment caused by insufficient aggregate demand, where wages and prices are sticky downward and markets fail to clear. Keynes argued that involuntary unemployment can persist because wages do not fall fast enough to restore equilibrium. (Ch. 11)

## L

**Labour force**
:   All persons of working age who are either employed or unemployed (actively seeking work). Persons who are neither employed nor seeking work (e.g., full-time students, retirees, discouraged workers) are classified as "not in the labour force." (Ch. 1)

**Labour force participation rate (LFPR)**
:   The fraction of the working-age population that is in the labour force: $\text{LFPR} = (\text{Employed} + \text{Unemployed}) / \text{Working-age population}$. (Ch. 1)

**Lorenz curve**
:   A graphical representation of the distribution of income or wages. The horizontal axis shows the cumulative percentage of workers (ranked from lowest to highest earners), and the vertical axis shows the cumulative percentage of total earnings. The further the Lorenz curve lies from the 45-degree line, the greater the inequality. (Ch. 7)

## M

**Mandated benefits**
:   Benefits that employers are legally required to provide to workers, such as health insurance, paid leave, or pension contributions. If workers value the benefit at its cost, mandated benefits are equivalent to a wage payment and have no employment effect. If workers value it at less than cost, the mandate acts like a tax on employment. (Ch. 4)

**Marginal product of labour (MPL)**
:   The additional output produced by hiring one more worker, holding all other inputs constant: $MP_L = \partial f(L,K) / \partial L$. Diminishing marginal returns imply that $MP_L$ falls as employment rises. (Ch. 3)

**Marginal rate of substitution (MRS)**
:   In the labour supply model, the rate at which a worker is willing to trade consumption for leisure while maintaining the same utility level. It equals the slope of the indifference curve and, at the optimum, equals the wage rate. (Ch. 2)

**Marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS)**
:   The rate at which a firm can substitute labour for capital while keeping output constant: $MRTS_{LK} = MP_L / MP_K$. It equals the slope of the isoquant. (Ch. 3)

**Marshall's rules of derived demand**
:   Four conditions under which the elasticity of demand for labour will be higher: (1) high elasticity of substitution between labour and capital, (2) high elasticity of demand for the firm's output, (3) large labour share of total costs, and (4) high elasticity of supply of other factors. (Ch. 3)

**Mincer earnings function**
:   A log-linear regression of earnings on years of schooling and potential experience (and its square): $\ln w = \alpha + \beta S + \gamma_1 X + \gamma_2 X^2 + \varepsilon$. The coefficient $\beta$ is interpreted as the rate of return to an additional year of schooling. Introduced by Jacob Mincer (1974). (Ch. 6)

**Minimum wage**
:   A legally mandated floor on the hourly wage rate. In the competitive model, a binding minimum wage (set above equilibrium) reduces employment. Empirical evidence suggests that moderate minimum wage increases have small to negligible disemployment effects. (Ch. 4)

**Monopsony**
:   A labour market structure in which there is a single buyer of labour (or, more broadly, where firms have wage-setting power due to search frictions, mobility costs, or thin markets). A monopsonist faces an upward-sloping labour supply curve and hires fewer workers at a lower wage than the competitive outcome. (Ch. 4)

**Moral hazard**
:   The tendency of an agent to exert less effort or take more risks when the consequences are borne partly by others. In employment, moral hazard arises when the firm cannot perfectly monitor worker effort, creating the need for incentive pay mechanisms. (Ch. 10)

## N

**NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment)**
:   The unemployment rate at which inflation remains stable. If unemployment falls below the NAIRU, wages rise faster than productivity, pushing up inflation. Closely related to the natural rate of unemployment. (Ch. 11)

**Natural rate of unemployment**
:   The long-run equilibrium unemployment rate determined by frictional and structural factors, after cyclical fluctuations are removed. It reflects the economy's institutional and demographic characteristics -- search efficiency, unemployment insurance generosity, labour market regulations, and demographic composition. (Ch. 11)

**Negative selection**
:   In the Roy model of migration, the pattern where migrants are drawn disproportionately from the lower tail of the income distribution in their origin country. Occurs when the destination country has a more compressed wage distribution (lower returns to skill). (Ch. 8)

**Non-labour income**
:   Income that a worker receives regardless of whether or how much they work, such as investment returns, government transfers, a spouse's earnings, or lottery winnings. An increase in non-labour income has a pure income effect, reducing labour supply. (Ch. 2)

## O

**Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition**
:   A statistical technique that decomposes the mean wage gap between two groups (e.g., men and women) into an "explained" component (due to differences in observable characteristics such as education and experience) and an "unexplained" component (which may reflect discrimination, unobserved skill differences, or both). (Ch. 9)

**Offer curve**
:   In the compensating differentials model, the locus of wage-amenity combinations offered by firms with different costs of providing amenities. The market offer curve is the outer envelope of individual firms' zero-profit isoprofit curves. (Ch. 5)

**On-the-job training (OJT)**
:   Training received while employed, which may be general (portable across firms) or specific (valuable only at the current firm). Becker's model predicts that workers pay for general training and firms pay for specific training. (Ch. 6)

**Opportunity cost**
:   The value of the next-best alternative forgone. In the context of education, the opportunity cost of attending college includes the earnings the student would have received had they worked instead. (Ch. 6)

**Own-wage elasticity of labour demand**
:   The percentage change in the quantity of labour demanded for a one-percent change in the wage of that same type of labour. Always negative (the demand curve slopes down). (Ch. 3)

## P

**Payroll tax**
:   A tax levied on wages, typically split between employers and employees (e.g., social security contributions). The economic incidence of the tax depends on the relative elasticities of labour supply and demand, not on the statutory split. (Ch. 4)

**Percentile ratio**
:   A measure of wage inequality defined as the ratio of earnings at one percentile to earnings at another. Common examples: the 90/10 ratio (top vs. bottom), 90/50 ratio (top vs. middle), and 50/10 ratio (middle vs. bottom). (Ch. 7)

**Phillips curve**
:   The empirical relationship, first documented by A.W. Phillips (1958), between the unemployment rate and the rate of wage (or price) inflation. The original curve suggested a stable trade-off; the expectations-augmented version (Friedman-Phelps) allows no long-run trade-off. (Ch. 11)

**Piece rate**
:   A compensation scheme in which the worker is paid a fixed amount per unit of output produced. Piece rates align individual incentives with firm objectives but may lead to quality problems or excessive risk-taking. (Ch. 10)

**Positive selection**
:   In the Roy model of migration, the pattern where migrants are drawn disproportionately from the upper tail of the income distribution in their origin country. Occurs when the destination country has a more dispersed wage distribution (higher returns to skill). (Ch. 8)

**Principal-agent problem**
:   A situation in which one party (the principal, typically the firm) delegates a task to another party (the agent, typically the worker) whose effort or actions cannot be perfectly observed. The firm must design a compensation contract that motivates the agent to act in the principal's interest. (Ch. 10)

**Production function**
:   A mathematical relationship describing the maximum output a firm can produce from given quantities of inputs: $q = f(L, K)$. It encodes the firm's technology. (Ch. 3)

## R

**Rate of return to education**
:   The internal rate of return on the investment in an additional year of schooling, accounting for both the direct costs (tuition) and indirect costs (foregone earnings) of education, relative to the lifetime earnings gain. In the Mincer framework, it is approximated by the coefficient on schooling. (Ch. 6)

**Reservation wage**
:   The minimum wage at which a worker is willing to accept employment. Below the reservation wage, the worker prefers not to work. In the labour-leisure model, the reservation wage equals the marginal rate of substitution between consumption and leisure evaluated at zero hours of work. (Ch. 2)

**Reservation wage (search theory)**
:   In the job search model, the wage at which a worker is indifferent between accepting a job offer and continuing to search. It depends on the cost of search, the distribution of available wage offers, the job finding rate, and the generosity of unemployment insurance. A higher UI benefit raises the reservation wage and lengthens the duration of unemployment. (Ch. 11)

**Residual inequality**
:   Wage inequality that remains after controlling for observable characteristics such as education, experience, occupation, and industry. Also called "within-group inequality." It has been the fastest-growing component of overall inequality since the 1980s. (Ch. 7)

**Roy model**
:   A model of self-selection developed by A.D. Roy (1951) in which individuals choose between sectors (or countries) based on their comparative advantage. Workers sort into the sector where their relative skills yield the highest return. Applied extensively to immigration (Ch. 8) and education decisions (Ch. 6). (Ch. 8)

## S

**Scale effect (demand side)**
:   The change in employment that results from a wage-induced change in the firm's output level. A higher wage raises marginal cost, leading the firm to produce less and therefore demand less of all inputs, including labour. The scale effect always reduces labour demand when the wage rises. (Ch. 3)

**Screening**
:   The use of observable signals (such as educational credentials) by employers to sort workers by unobservable productivity. In the screening hypothesis, education may not increase productivity but serves as a filter. Related to but distinct from signaling. (Ch. 6)

**Search theory**
:   A class of models that explain unemployment as the outcome of a costly, time-consuming process of matching workers to jobs. Workers sequentially sample wage offers and accept any offer above their reservation wage. The optimal search strategy trades off the expected gain from continued search against the cost of remaining unemployed. (Ch. 11)

**Self-selection**
:   The non-random sorting of individuals into different states (employed/not employed, migrant/stayer, educated/uneducated) based on their unobserved characteristics. Self-selection creates econometric challenges because the observed group is not a random sample of the population. (Ch. 8)

**Separation rate**
:   The probability that an employed worker loses or leaves their job within a given period. Together with the job finding rate, it determines the steady-state unemployment rate: $u^* = s / (s + f)$. (Ch. 11)

**Shirking model**
:   An efficiency wage model in which firms pay above-market wages to deter workers from shirking (exerting low effort). If caught shirking, the worker is fired and suffers a loss equal to the wage premium. The higher the wage premium, the less likely the worker is to shirk. Equilibrium unemployment serves as a discipline device. (Ch. 10)

**Signaling**
:   The Spence (1973) model in which workers use costly, observable actions (such as obtaining a degree) to credibly convey their unobservable productivity to employers. For signaling to work, the cost of obtaining the signal must be lower for high-ability workers than for low-ability workers. (Ch. 6)

**Skill-biased technological change (SBTC)**
:   Technological innovation that increases the relative demand for skilled workers, thereby raising the wage premium for education and contributing to wage inequality. SBTC is the leading explanation for the rising college wage premium since the 1980s. (Ch. 7)

**Specific capital (firm-specific human capital)**
:   Skills or knowledge that are valuable only at the current firm and have no value elsewhere. Specific capital creates a bilateral monopoly between the worker and the firm, reduces turnover, and is typically financed through wage sharing. (Ch. 8)

**Specific training**
:   Training that increases a worker's productivity only at the current firm. Under Becker's framework, firms and workers share both the cost of and the return to specific training, creating mutual incentives to maintain the employment relationship. (Ch. 6)

**Statistical discrimination**
:   Discrimination that arises when employers use group-level statistical averages (e.g., average productivity by gender or race) as a proxy for individual productivity when individual information is costly or unavailable. Unlike taste-based discrimination, it is rooted in information problems rather than prejudice. (Ch. 9)

**Structural unemployment**
:   Unemployment caused by a mismatch between the skills or location of workers and the requirements or location of available jobs. It arises from long-term structural changes in the economy, such as technological change, industry decline, or shifts in the geographic distribution of production. (Ch. 11)

**Substitutes (in production)**
:   Two inputs are substitutes if an increase in the quantity (or decrease in the price) of one reduces the demand for the other. For example, automated checkout machines and cashiers may be substitutes. (Ch. 3)

**Substitution effect (labour demand)**
:   In the long-run labour demand framework, the tendency of a firm to replace a more expensive input with a cheaper one. When wages rise, the firm substitutes capital for labour along the isoquant. The substitution effect always reduces the quantity of labour demanded when wages rise. (Ch. 3)

**Substitution effect (labour supply)**
:   The change in hours worked caused by a change in the wage rate, holding utility constant. A higher wage raises the opportunity cost of leisure, making work relatively more attractive and increasing labour supply. The substitution effect always leads to more hours worked when the wage rises. (Ch. 2)

**Superstar effect**
:   The phenomenon, analyzed by Rosen (1981), in which a small number of individuals in a profession capture a disproportionately large share of total earnings, driven by imperfect substitution among performers and technology that amplifies small differences in talent. (Ch. 7)

## T

**Taste-based discrimination**
:   Discrimination arising from personal prejudice, as modeled by Becker (1957). An employer with a taste for discrimination against a group acts as if hiring members of that group involves an additional cost (the discrimination coefficient $d$), leading to lower wages or fewer employment opportunities for that group. (Ch. 9)

**Tax incidence**
:   The distribution of the economic burden of a tax between buyers and sellers (or, in labour markets, between firms and workers). The statutory incidence (who writes the cheque) differs from the economic incidence (whose real income falls). The more inelastic side of the market bears a larger share of the tax burden. (Ch. 4)

**Time rate**
:   A compensation scheme in which the worker is paid a fixed amount per unit of time (hourly, weekly, monthly) regardless of output. Time rates are simpler to administer but provide weaker effort incentives than piece rates. (Ch. 10)

**Tournament**
:   A compensation scheme in which workers compete against each other for a prize (e.g., a promotion or bonus), and pay depends on relative performance rather than absolute output. Tournaments can elicit high effort but may also encourage sabotage or excessive risk-taking. (Ch. 10)

## U

**Underemployment**
:   A situation in which a worker is employed but either works fewer hours than desired (time-related underemployment) or holds a job that does not fully utilise their skills and education (skills-related underemployment). (Ch. 1)

**Unemployment insurance (UI)**
:   Government-provided income support to workers who have lost their jobs involuntarily and are actively searching for new employment. UI raises the reservation wage and may extend unemployment duration, but it also enables better job matching by allowing workers to search more effectively. (Ch. 11)

**Unemployment rate**
:   The fraction of the labour force that is unemployed: $u = \text{Unemployed} / \text{Labour force}$. The official rate counts only those who are actively searching for work and available for work during the reference period. (Ch. 1)

## V

**Value of marginal product (VMP)**
:   The monetary value of the additional output produced by one more unit of labour: $VMP_L = p \times MP_L$, where $p$ is the output price. A profit-maximizing competitive firm hires labour until $VMP_L = w$. (Ch. 3)

**Value of a statistical life (VSL)**
:   The implicit value that labour market participants place on small changes in the probability of death, estimated from the wage premium associated with risky jobs. If workers in a job with 1/10,000 higher annual fatality risk earn Rs. 5,000 more per year, the implied VSL is Rs. 5 crore. (Ch. 5)

## W

**Wage compression**
:   A situation in which wages are more equal than worker productivities, typically arising from institutional factors (minimum wages, unions) or monopsony power. Wage compression can encourage firms to provide more training, since they capture a larger share of the return. (Ch. 6)

**Wage-tenure profile**
:   The relationship between a worker's wage and the length of time spent with the current employer. Upward-sloping wage-tenure profiles may reflect returns to firm-specific human capital, deferred compensation, or matching effects. (Ch. 8)

**Within-group inequality**
:   Wage variation among workers with the same observable characteristics (education, experience, occupation). Also called residual inequality. Its growth since the 1980s suggests that unobservable factors (ability, effort, luck, firm-specific rents) have become more important in determining wages. (Ch. 7)

---

*Terms are defined as used in Borjas, G.J., Labor Economics, 8th edition. Chapter references indicate where each concept is primarily introduced.*

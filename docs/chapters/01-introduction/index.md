# 1. Introduction to Labor Economics

**Borjas Chapter 1 | 2 lecture hours**

> *"Labour is the source of all wealth, the political economists assert. And it really is the source -- next to nature, which supplies it with the material that it converts into wealth."* -- Friedrich Engels, *Dialectics of Nature* (1883)

Most of us will spend roughly half our waking adult lives working. The wages we earn from that work determine where we live, what we eat, how we educate our children, and -- for better or worse -- much of our social identity. Labour economics is the study of these outcomes: how wages are determined, who works and who does not, how firms decide whom to hire and how much to pay, and what happens when governments intervene. It sits at the intersection of microeconomics, public policy, and everyday life.

---

## Why Study Labour Economics?

Labour markets are distinctive in at least three ways that separate them from other markets in economics.

**First, the "commodity" is inseparable from the person.** When a firm buys steel, it does not care about the steel's feelings, commute time, or career aspirations. When a firm hires a worker, all of these matter. Employment relationships are not spot transactions -- they involve ongoing interactions, trust, monitoring, and motivation. This is why labour economics devotes entire chapters to topics such as discrimination, compensating wage differentials, and incentive pay.

**Second, labour income is the dominant source of household income.** In most economies, wages and salaries account for roughly two-thirds of national income. Changes in the labour market therefore ripple immediately into consumption, savings, health, and inequality.

**Third, governments intervene heavily in labour markets.** Minimum wages, employment protection legislation, payroll taxes, unemployment insurance, affirmative action, and immigration policy all shape labour market outcomes in ways that have no close parallel in, say, the market for wheat.

## The Labour Market as a Market

Labour economics applies the standard tools of supply and demand, but adapts them to the peculiarities of work.

!!! model "The Basic Framework"

    - **Labour supply** comes from workers (households) who decide whether to work, how many hours to supply, and what occupation and location to choose. These decisions depend on wages, non-labour income, preferences for leisure, and household characteristics.

    - **Labour demand** comes from firms that hire workers to produce output. Firms choose how many workers to hire by comparing the revenue a worker generates (the marginal revenue product) to the cost of hiring (the wage plus benefits and taxes).

    - **Equilibrium** is reached when the quantity of labour supplied equals the quantity demanded. In equilibrium, the wage rate adjusts to clear the market -- at least in the competitive benchmark.

Of course, real labour markets rarely reach a single, frictionless equilibrium. Workers search for jobs. Firms screen applicants. Unions bargain collectively. Minimum wages impose floors. These complications are what make labour economics rich and practically relevant.

## Key Facts About Labour Markets

Before diving into theory, it helps to have a few empirical benchmarks in mind.

!!! empirical "Stylised Facts for the United States (Borjas Ch. 1)"

    1. **Labour force participation has shifted.** Male LFP has declined gradually from about 86% in 1950 to under 70% today, while female LFP rose sharply from 34% to a peak of about 60% in the late 1990s before plateauing.

    2. **Hours of work have fallen over the long run.** The average workweek declined from about 60 hours in 1900 to roughly 34 hours today, though this decline has stalled since the 1970s.

    3. **Real wages grew rapidly from 1945 to 1973, then stagnated for many workers.** The college wage premium -- the earnings gap between college and high-school graduates -- has widened dramatically since 1980.

    4. **Unemployment fluctuates with the business cycle** but averages around 5-6% in the US. Certain demographic groups (young workers, minorities) face persistently higher unemployment rates.

    5. **Earnings inequality has increased.** The 90/10 wage ratio (the ratio of earnings at the 90th percentile to earnings at the 10th percentile) has grown substantially since the late 1970s.

## Actors in the Labour Market

Borjas identifies three key actors whose decisions jointly determine labour market outcomes:

### Workers

Workers make decisions about:

- **Whether to work** (the participation decision)
- **How many hours to work** (the hours decision)
- **What kind of work to do** (occupation, industry, location)
- **How much education and training to acquire** (human capital investment)

### Firms

Firms make decisions about:

- **How many workers to hire** (the employment decision)
- **What wage to offer** (the compensation decision)
- **How to organise production** (capital-labour substitution)
- **Where to locate** (geographic labour demand)

### Government

Governments shape the rules of the game through:

- **Taxes** (income taxes, payroll taxes)
- **Transfers** (unemployment insurance, welfare, earned income credits)
- **Regulations** (minimum wages, occupational safety, anti-discrimination law)
- **Immigration policy** (who can enter the labour market)
- **Education policy** (public schooling, student loans)

!!! policy "Government as a Major Employer"

    In many countries, the government is itself one of the largest employers. In India, public sector employment (central, state, and local government plus public enterprises) accounts for roughly 17-18 million jobs -- a small share of total employment but a disproportionately important source of formal, secure, well-paying jobs. Understanding government employment decisions is itself a labour economics question.

## Positive versus Normative Economics

Labour economics, like all economics, distinguishes between **positive** statements (what *is*) and **normative** statements (what *ought to be*).

!!! model "Positive vs. Normative"

    - **Positive:** "A 10% increase in the minimum wage reduces teenage employment by 1-3%." This is a testable empirical claim.

    - **Normative:** "The government should raise the minimum wage to reduce poverty." This involves a value judgement about the trade-off between higher wages for some and possible job losses for others.

    Good labour economics keeps the two distinct. We use positive analysis to understand the *consequences* of policies, and normative analysis to evaluate whether those consequences are desirable.

Much of the debate in labour policy arises because people agree on the positive analysis but disagree on normative priorities -- or because they disagree on the positive analysis itself. The minimum wage debate is a perfect example: economists continue to argue about the employment effects, and even those who agree on the magnitudes may disagree about whether the trade-off is worth it.

Borjas organises the textbook in a logical progression. Here is a preview of the eleven chapters we will cover this semester:

Chapters 2-4 form the core supply-demand-equilibrium framework. Chapters 5-10 apply and extend that framework to specific topics. Chapter 11 addresses the macroeconomic intersection.

## India's Labour Market: A First Look

!!! indian "India's Labour Force -- Size, Composition, and Data Sources"

    India's labour market is one of the largest and most complex in the world. A few headline facts to orient us:

    - **Size:** India's working-age population (15+) exceeds 1 billion. The labour force -- those who are either employed or actively seeking work -- numbers roughly 560-580 million as of the latest Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS 2023-24).

    - **Agriculture dominates.** Despite contributing only about 15% of GDP, agriculture still employs over 40% of the workforce. This structural imbalance is one of the defining features of the Indian labour market.

    - **Informality is pervasive.** Over 80% of workers are in informal employment, lacking written contracts, social security, or legal protections. This makes standard labour market frameworks (designed for formal wage employment) only partially applicable.

    - **Female LFPR is low.** India's female labour force participation rate, at roughly 37% (usual status, PLFS 2023-24), is among the lowest in the world for a country at its income level. Understanding why is one of the most important questions in Indian labour economics.

    - **Data source -- the PLFS:** The Periodic Labour Force Survey, conducted by the National Statistical Office (NSO), is India's primary source of labour market data. It provides quarterly estimates for urban areas and annual estimates for both urban and rural areas. We will use PLFS data throughout this course.

    Throughout this course, we will repeatedly ask: *How well does the Borjas framework -- developed primarily for the US labour market -- apply to India?* Sometimes it applies directly; sometimes it needs significant adaptation. That tension is pedagogically valuable.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Labour economics** studies how labour markets determine wages, employment, and the allocation of workers to jobs.

2. Labour markets are distinctive because the "commodity" (labour) is inseparable from the person, labour income dominates household budgets, and governments intervene heavily.

3. The basic framework involves **three actors** -- workers, firms, and governments -- whose decisions interact to determine equilibrium outcomes.

4. **Positive analysis** asks what happens when conditions change; **normative analysis** asks whether the outcome is desirable. Keeping the two distinct is essential.

5. The textbook progresses from core supply-demand theory (Chapters 2-4) to applications (Chapters 5-10) to unemployment (Chapter 11).

6. India's labour market is characterised by its massive scale, agricultural dominance, pervasive informality, and low female participation -- features we will examine throughout the course.

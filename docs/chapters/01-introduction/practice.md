## Conceptual Questions

??? question "Q1. What distinguishes labour markets from other markets in economics?"

    Labour markets differ from goods markets in at least three fundamental ways:

    1. **Inseparability of worker and service.** Unlike a commodity like steel or wheat, the "product" (labour services) cannot be separated from the person providing it. This means that working conditions, location, commute time, workplace culture, and interpersonal relationships all matter -- they are part of the transaction. This gives rise to topics like compensating differentials (Ch. 5) and discrimination (Ch. 9).

    2. **Labour income dominates household budgets.** Wages and salaries typically account for about two-thirds of national income in most economies. This means that labour market outcomes directly determine living standards, inequality, and social mobility.

    3. **Pervasive government intervention.** Governments regulate labour markets far more intensively than most product markets -- through minimum wages, employment protection, payroll taxes, anti-discrimination laws, immigration controls, and unemployment insurance.

??? question "Q2. Define the following terms: (a) labour force, (b) labour force participation rate, (c) unemployment rate."

    **(a) Labour force:** The labour force consists of all persons of working age who are either *employed* (currently working for pay or profit) or *unemployed* (not working but actively searching for work and available to work). It excludes those who are neither working nor looking for work (e.g., full-time students, retirees, discouraged workers who have stopped searching).

    **(b) Labour force participation rate (LFPR):** The fraction of the working-age population that is in the labour force.

    \[
    \text{LFPR} = \frac{\text{Labour Force}}{\text{Working-Age Population}} \times 100
    \]

    **(c) Unemployment rate:** The fraction of the labour force that is unemployed.

    \[
    \text{Unemployment Rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labour Force}} \times 100
    \]

    Note the denominator: the unemployment rate is the share of the *labour force* (not the entire population) that is jobless and looking for work.

??? question "Q3. Explain the difference between positive and normative statements in labour economics. Classify each of the following: (a) 'Raising the minimum wage to Rs. 300/day would reduce employment in the garment sector.' (b) 'The government should raise the minimum wage to help low-income families.' (c) 'Women with more education are more likely to participate in the labour force.'"

    **Positive statements** describe *what is* or *what would happen*. They are, in principle, testable with data. **Normative statements** express *what ought to be* -- they involve value judgements.

    - **(a) Positive.** This is a claim about the causal effect of a policy change on employment. It can be tested empirically (even if the empirical evidence is contested).

    - **(b) Normative.** The word "should" is a signal. This statement involves a value judgement about the trade-off between higher wages for some workers and possible job losses for others.

    - **(c) Positive.** This is a testable empirical regularity about the correlation between education and labour force participation.

??? question "Q4. Why is it important for economists to distinguish between positive and normative analysis when evaluating labour market policies?"

    The distinction matters for three reasons:

    1. **Clarity of debate.** Many policy disagreements arise because participants conflate empirical claims with value judgements. Separating the two allows us to identify exactly *where* people disagree -- is it about what the effect of a policy would be (positive), or about whether that effect is desirable (normative)?

    2. **Comparative advantage.** Economists have a comparative advantage in positive analysis -- estimating the effects of policies using data and theory. Whether those effects are *desirable* is ultimately a question for democratic deliberation, not economic expertise alone.

    3. **Avoiding motivated reasoning.** When positive and normative analysis are mixed, there is a temptation to shade the positive analysis to support one's normative priors. Keeping them separate is a discipline against this.

## Labour Force Concepts

??? question "Q5. In a country with a working-age population of 800 million, 500 million are employed and 40 million are unemployed. Calculate: (a) the labour force, (b) the LFPR, (c) the unemployment rate, and (d) the number of persons not in the labour force."

    **(a) Labour force** = Employed + Unemployed = 500 + 40 = **540 million**

    **(b) LFPR** = Labour Force / Working-Age Population = 540 / 800 = **67.5%**

    **(c) Unemployment rate** = Unemployed / Labour Force = 40 / 540 = **7.4%**

    **(d) Not in the labour force** = Working-Age Population - Labour Force = 800 - 540 = **260 million**

    These 260 million include full-time students, homemakers, retirees, the chronically ill, and discouraged workers who have stopped searching.

??? question "Q6. Explain the three labour market states (employed, unemployed, not in the labour force) and describe the six possible flows between them. Why do these flows matter for understanding labour markets?"

    The three states and six flows are:

    | Flow | From | To | Example |
    |------|------|----|---------|
    | 1 | Employed | Unemployed | Worker is laid off, starts searching |
    | 2 | Unemployed | Employed | Jobseeker finds and accepts a job |
    | 3 | Employed | Not in LF | Worker retires or leaves to raise children |
    | 4 | Not in LF | Employed | Homemaker re-enters and finds a job directly |
    | 5 | Unemployed | Not in LF | Discouraged worker stops searching |
    | 6 | Not in LF | Unemployed | Student graduates and starts job search |

    These flows matter because **aggregate stocks (employment, unemployment, NILF) are the net result of these underlying flows.** Two economies can have the same unemployment rate but very different labour market dynamics. For example, an economy with high inflows into and out of unemployment is more "fluid" than one where unemployment is stagnant. Flow data also reveal phenomena like the **discouraged worker effect** (flow 5) and the **added worker effect** (flow 6 triggered by a spouse's job loss).

??? question "Q7. The female labour force participation rate in India (~37%) is much lower than in the United States (~57%) or China (~61%). List three possible explanations for India's low female LFPR. For each, state whether it is a supply-side or demand-side explanation."

    1. **Social norms and cultural constraints (supply-side).** In many Indian communities, there is a social stigma attached to women working outside the home, particularly in certain types of jobs. As household income rises, families may "afford" to withdraw women from the labour force as a marker of social status -- the so-called "income effect" or "sanskritisation" hypothesis.

    2. **Lack of suitable jobs (demand-side).** The Indian economy may not generate enough jobs that women are willing or able to take, given constraints on mobility, safety, and working conditions. The decline of manufacturing and the slow pace of structural transformation limit demand for female labour.

    3. **Rising female education (supply-side, short-run).** As more women enrol in secondary and higher education, they are temporarily out of the labour force. Over the long run, education tends to *increase* LFPR, but in the short run, the enrolment effect reduces measured participation.

    Other valid explanations include: lack of childcare infrastructure (supply-side), measurement issues with unpaid domestic and care work (data issue), safety concerns during commuting (supply-side constraint), and the decline of household-based manufacturing (demand-side).

## Short-Answer and Application

??? question "Q8. Consider a worker who is employed part-time at a low wage but wants full-time work and cannot find it. Is this person 'employed' or 'unemployed' according to the official labour force definition? What are the limitations of the official classification in this case?"

    According to the official definition (used by both the US Bureau of Labor Statistics and India's PLFS), this person is **classified as employed** -- anyone who worked for even one hour during the reference period is considered employed.

    The limitation is clear: the official classification does not distinguish between adequate and inadequate employment. This person is **underemployed** -- they have a job but want and are available for more work. Underemployment is a particularly important concept in developing countries like India, where:

    - Many workers are engaged in low-productivity, low-hours work in agriculture or the informal sector
    - The PLFS uses the concept of the "usual principal activity status" (most of the year) and "current weekly status" to capture different dimensions, but underemployment remains hard to measure fully

    The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes alternative measures (U-1 through U-6) that capture different degrees of labour underutilisation. The PLFS similarly distinguishes between usual status and current weekly status to better capture underemployment.

??? question "Q9. Borjas writes that labour economists study the 'price' and 'quantity' outcomes of the labour market. What is the 'price' and what is the 'quantity' in a labour market? How do these differ from prices and quantities in a product market like the market for rice?"

    In a labour market:

    - **Price** = the **wage rate** (the price per unit of labour services, typically per hour or per month)
    - **Quantity** = the **amount of labour** (measured as the number of workers employed, total hours worked, or some measure of labour input)

    These differ from product markets in several important ways:

    1. **The "seller" (worker) cares about the transaction itself**, not just the payment. A rice farmer does not care who eats the rice, but a worker cares deeply about who they work for and under what conditions.

    2. **The price (wage) is often negotiated or set by institutional rules** (minimum wages, collective bargaining, grade-pay scales), rather than being determined purely by market clearing.

    3. **The quantity is not purely physical.** Labour effort -- how hard someone works -- is not directly observable or contractable, creating principal-agent problems that do not arise in commodity markets.

    4. **Duration and attachment matter.** Labour is typically traded in ongoing relationships (jobs), not one-off spot transactions.

??? question "Q10. India's National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (NREGA) guarantees 100 days of unskilled manual work per year to every rural household. Using the concepts from this chapter, explain how NREGA affects (a) the labour force participation rate, (b) the unemployment rate, and (c) wages in rural labour markets."

    **(a) LFPR:** NREGA is likely to **increase** the rural LFPR. By providing a guaranteed employment option, it draws people who were previously not in the labour force (particularly women and marginal workers during the lean agricultural season) into the labour force. They now have a realistic prospect of finding work, so they participate.

    **(b) Unemployment rate:** The effect on the unemployment rate is ambiguous. On one hand, NREGA creates jobs, which should reduce unemployment directly. On the other hand, by drawing new participants into the labour force (who may not all get NREGA work immediately), it could keep the measured unemployment rate from falling as much as expected.

    **(c) Rural wages:** NREGA effectively sets a **wage floor** in rural labour markets. If the NREGA wage is above the prevailing market wage for unskilled labour, private employers must raise their wages to attract workers -- this is a standard labour supply effect. Empirical evidence (e.g., Imbert and Papp, 2015; Muralidharan, Niehaus, and Sukhtankar, 2016) finds significant positive effects of NREGA on private-sector wages in rural India.

    In the framework of this chapter, NREGA is an example of government intervention that simultaneously affects labour supply (through participation incentives), labour demand (through public employment), and equilibrium wages.

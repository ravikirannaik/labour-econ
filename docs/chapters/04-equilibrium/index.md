# 4. Labor Market Equilibrium

<span class="chapter-tag hours-tag">3 Hours</span> <span class="chapter-tag borjas-tag">Borjas Ch. 4</span>

Chapters 2 and 3 built the two blades of Alfred Marshall's scissors: labour supply (workers choosing how much to work) and labour demand (firms choosing how many workers to hire). This chapter brings the blades together. **Equilibrium** occurs where supply equals demand, determining the market wage and employment level simultaneously. But the real analytical power of the equilibrium framework lies in what happens when policy intervenes---through taxes, immigration, or mandated benefits---and in understanding how markets adjust over time.

---

## Equilibrium in a Single Competitive Labour Market

A **competitive labour market equilibrium** occurs at the wage \(w^*\) and employment level \(L^*\) where the quantity of labour supplied equals the quantity demanded:

\[
L^S(w^*) = L^D(w^*) = L^*
\]

!!! model "Equilibrium Conditions"
    At equilibrium:

    - Every worker willing to work at wage \(w^*\) finds a job.
    - Every firm willing to hire at wage \(w^*\) finds workers.
    - There is no upward or downward pressure on the wage.

    If \(w > w^*\), there is a **surplus** of labour (unemployment): more people want to work than firms want to hire, and competition among workers pushes the wage down. If \(w < w^*\), there is a **shortage**: firms compete for scarce workers, bidding the wage up.

Graphically, equilibrium is the intersection of the upward-sloping supply curve and the downward-sloping demand curve in \((L, w)\) space.

---

## Efficiency of the Competitive Equilibrium

The competitive equilibrium maximizes the **total gains from trade** in the labour market---the sum of the **producer surplus** (the gap between what firms earn from a worker and what they pay) and the **worker surplus** (the gap between what workers are paid and their reservation wage).

!!! model "Welfare Analysis"
    - **Worker surplus** is the area below the wage line and above the supply curve.
    - **Producer surplus** (firm surplus) is the area above the wage line and below the demand curve.
    - **Total surplus** = Worker surplus + Producer surplus.

    At the competitive equilibrium, total surplus is maximized. Any deviation from \(w^*\)---whether from a minimum wage, a tax, or a quantity restriction---creates **deadweight loss**: surplus that is destroyed because some mutually beneficial employment relationships no longer occur.

This result is a specific application of the First Welfare Theorem: under perfect competition, the market allocation is Pareto efficient. Of course, efficiency does not imply equity, and much of labour market policy involves trading off some efficiency for distributional goals.

---

## The Cobweb Model

Labour markets do not adjust instantaneously. The **cobweb model** describes how markets with lagged supply responses can exhibit cyclical adjustments or even instability.

Consider a professional labour market (e.g., engineers or doctors) where supply responds with a lag---it takes years to train new workers. The sequence of adjustment is:

1. High wages today attract many students into the field.
2. Several years later, a large cohort enters the market, increasing supply.
3. The surge in supply depresses wages.
4. Low wages discourage new entrants.
5. Years later, a small cohort enters, and wages rise again.

!!! model "Cobweb Stability Conditions"
    The cobweb converges to equilibrium (is stable) when the supply curve is **steeper** than the demand curve---that is, when supply is relatively inelastic compared to demand. In this case, each oscillation is smaller than the previous one.

    The cobweb is **unstable** (explosive) when the supply curve is flatter than the demand curve. Each cycle produces larger wage swings.

    The cobweb model helps explain why certain occupations (e.g., engineers in the 1980s, nurses periodically) experience recurring cycles of shortage and surplus.

## Policy Application: Payroll Taxes

Governments levy payroll taxes to fund social insurance programs. In India, the Employees' Provident Fund (EPF) and Employees' State Insurance (ESI) are major examples. A key question is: **Who actually bears the burden of the tax?**

### Statutory vs. Economic Incidence

Suppose the government imposes a payroll tax of \(t\) per worker, levied on the employer. The statutory incidence falls on the firm. But the **economic incidence**---who actually pays---depends on the elasticities of supply and demand.

!!! model "Tax Incidence Analysis"
    Before the tax, equilibrium is at \((L^*, w^*)\). After an employer-side payroll tax of \(t\):

    - The firm's cost of hiring a worker is \(w + t\), so the demand curve shifts **down** by \(t\) (the firm is willing to pay \(t\) less in wages for each worker).
    - The new equilibrium wage \(w_1\) falls below \(w^*\).
    - The firm pays a total cost of \(w_1 + t\) per worker.

    The tax burden is shared:

    - **Workers bear**: \(w^* - w_1\) (the drop in their wage).
    - **Firms bear**: \(t - (w^* - w_1)\) (the increase in their total labour cost).

    **Key insight:** The share of the tax borne by workers is larger when labour supply is **inelastic** (workers cannot easily leave the market). The share borne by firms is larger when labour demand is **inelastic**. In the extreme case of perfectly inelastic supply (vertical supply curve), workers bear the entire tax regardless of who writes the check.

    **Statutory incidence is irrelevant** for economic outcomes. A tax on employers and a tax on employees of the same amount produce the same equilibrium wage, employment, and tax burden sharing.

!!! indian "EPF and ESI in India"
    India's two major payroll tax programs illustrate these principles:

    - **Employees' Provident Fund (EPF):** Both employer and employee contribute 12% of basic wages. Applies to establishments with 20+ employees and workers earning up to Rs. 15,000/month (the statutory wage ceiling). The total contribution is 24% of basic wages.
    - **Employees' State Insurance (ESI):** Employer contributes 3.25% and employee contributes 0.75% of wages. Applies to establishments with 10+ employees and workers earning up to Rs. 21,000/month.

    The economic incidence theory predicts that much of the employer's share is shifted onto workers through lower wages. Empirical evidence from developing countries supports this: **Kugler and Kugler (2009)** found in Colombia that a 10-percentage-point increase in payroll taxes reduced wages by 1.5--2.4%, consistent with substantial pass-through to workers. In India, the relatively inelastic supply of formal-sector workers (who value the benefits of formal employment) suggests that workers bear a significant portion of EPF/ESI contributions.

    An important institutional nuance: because EPF and ESI apply only to firms above a size threshold, these payroll taxes create a **discontinuity** at the threshold, giving small firms an incentive to remain below it or to hire informal workers. This contributes to India's "missing middle" in firm size distribution.

## Policy Application: Immigration

Immigration increases the supply of labour in the receiving market. The Borjas framework analyses the welfare consequences.

### The Immigration Surplus

!!! model "Immigration in the Competitive Model"
    Suppose the labour market is initially in equilibrium at \((L_0, w_0)\). An inflow of \(M\) immigrants shifts the labour supply curve to the right.

    At the new equilibrium:

    - The wage falls from \(w_0\) to \(w_1\).
    - Total employment rises from \(L_0\) to \(L_1 = L_0 + M'\), where \(M' \leq M\) because some natives may withdraw from the labour force.

    **Winners and losers:**

    - **Employers** gain: they hire more workers at a lower wage. Producer surplus increases.
    - **Native workers** lose: they earn a lower wage. Worker surplus of natives decreases.
    - **Immigrants** gain: they earn \(w_1\), which presumably exceeds what they earned in their home country.

    The **immigration surplus** is the net gain to the native population (employer gains minus native worker losses). Geometrically, it is the small triangle between the old and new supply curves, below the demand curve, and above the new wage:

    \[
    \text{Immigration Surplus} \approx \frac{1}{2} \times \eta_D \times s_L \times m^2 \times Q_0
    \]

    where \(\eta_D\) is the absolute value of the labour demand elasticity, \(s_L\) is labour's share of national income, \(m\) is the immigration rate (immigrants as a fraction of the native workforce), and \(Q_0\) is initial GDP.

    Borjas estimates the immigration surplus for the US at roughly **0.1--0.3% of GDP**---positive but small. The key implication: immigration is primarily a **redistribution** from native workers to employers, with a small net gain.

!!! empirical "Card (1990): The Mariel Boatlift"
    In 1980, Fidel Castro allowed 125,000 Cubans to emigrate from the port of Mariel. About 45,000 settled in Miami, increasing Miami's labour force by roughly 7% almost overnight. David Card (1990) compared Miami's labour market outcomes with those of four comparison cities (Atlanta, Houston, Los Angeles, Tampa-St. Petersburg) that did not receive the influx.

    Card found **no significant effect** on the wages or unemployment rates of non-Cuban workers in Miami, even among low-skilled workers who would compete most directly with the Mariel immigrants. This has been interpreted as evidence that local labour markets can absorb large immigration shocks, possibly because demand adjusts (firms expand, new firms enter) and because immigrants and natives are imperfect substitutes.

    Borjas (2017) later re-examined the Mariel Boatlift, focusing on the subset of workers without a high school degree, and found significant wage declines. The debate between Card and Borjas on this episode illustrates how empirical conclusions can depend on the choice of comparison group, sample restrictions, and outcome variable.

## Policy Application: Mandated Benefits

Governments often require employers to provide benefits such as health insurance, maternity leave, or severance pay. Economic analysis treats mandated benefits similarly to payroll taxes, but with an important twist.

!!! model "Mandated Benefits and the Labour Market"
    A mandated benefit that costs the employer \(c\) per worker shifts the demand curve down by \(c\), just like a payroll tax of \(c\). **However**, if workers value the benefit, the supply curve shifts **down** (rightward) as well---workers are willing to accept a lower wage in exchange for the benefit.

    Let \(b\) denote the value workers place on the benefit. Three cases arise:

    - If \(b = c\) (workers value the benefit at its full cost): the supply curve shifts down by exactly \(c\), the wage falls by \(c\), employment is **unchanged**, and there is **no deadweight loss**. Workers effectively pay for the benefit through lower wages.
    - If \(b < c\) (workers value the benefit less than it costs): the wage falls by less than \(c\), employment declines, and there is deadweight loss. This is the case for benefits that some workers do not want or need.
    - If \(b = 0\) (workers place no value on the benefit): the mandated benefit is equivalent to a pure payroll tax, with the same employment and deadweight loss effects.

!!! empirical "Gruber (1994): Maternity Benefits"
    Jonathan Gruber studied the effect of US state mandates requiring health insurance coverage for maternity. He found that wages of women of childbearing age **fell by approximately the cost of the mandate**, while their employment did not decline significantly. This is consistent with the case where \(b \approx c\): women valued the maternity coverage and accepted lower wages in exchange. The mandate effectively converted explicit wages into an in-kind benefit, with minimal efficiency loss.

!!! policy "Implications for Indian Labour Policy"
    India mandates numerous benefits for formal-sector workers: EPF, ESI, gratuity (Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972), and maternity leave (Maternity Benefit Act, 1961, amended 2017 to provide 26 weeks of paid leave). The theory predicts:

    - To the extent workers value these benefits, wages will adjust downward and employment effects will be small.
    - Benefits that workers do not value (or that apply to workers who already have alternatives) will function like a tax, reducing formal employment.
    - The high cost of mandated benefits in the formal sector is widely cited as one reason for India's low rate of formal employment. Firms avoid crossing regulatory thresholds to escape these mandates, contributing to the "missing middle" and persistent informality.

## The Triangle of Deadweight Loss

A unifying theme across all three policy applications is the concept of **deadweight loss (DWL)**---the reduction in total surplus caused by a policy that moves the market away from the competitive equilibrium.

!!! model "Deadweight Loss"
    For a tax or binding price floor/ceiling, the DWL is the area of the triangle between the supply and demand curves, over the range of employment that is eliminated:

    \[
    DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times \Delta L \times \Delta w
    \]

    where \(\Delta L\) is the employment reduction and \(\Delta w\) is the difference between the demand price and the supply price at the new (reduced) level of employment.

    Key properties:

    - DWL is **zero** at the competitive equilibrium.
    - DWL increases with the **square** of the tax (or the distance of the price floor from equilibrium), making large distortions disproportionately costly.
    - DWL is larger when supply and demand are **more elastic**---because elastic curves mean larger quantity responses.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The competitive equilibrium, where \(L^S = L^D\), maximizes total surplus in the labour market. Departures from this equilibrium create deadweight loss.
2. The economic incidence of a payroll tax depends on the relative elasticities of supply and demand, **not** on who writes the check. With inelastic labour supply, workers bear most of the tax.
3. Immigration redistributes income from native workers to employers, with a small positive net gain (the immigration surplus). Empirical evidence suggests that even large immigration inflows may have modest wage effects.
4. Mandated benefits are less costly than equivalent taxes when workers value the benefits. If \(b = c\), the mandate is fully efficient; if \(b < c\), it generates deadweight loss.
5. The cobweb model explains why labour markets with training lags can exhibit cyclical patterns of shortage and surplus.

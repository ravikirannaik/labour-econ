## Key Papers

!!! empirical "Core Labour Supply Theory and Evidence"

    - **Blundell, Richard and Thomas MaCurdy.** "Labor Supply: A Review of Alternative Approaches." In *Handbook of Labor Economics*, Vol. 3A, edited by O. Ashenfelter and D. Card, Elsevier, 1999, pp. 1559-1695. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1573-4463(99)03008-4)
        - *The definitive survey of labour supply estimation. Covers static and life-cycle models, identification strategies, and the range of estimated elasticities. Essential reference for understanding the empirical landscape.*

    - **Chetty, Raj, Adam Guren, Day Manoli, and Andrea Weber.** "Are Micro and Macro Labor Supply Elasticities Consistent? A Review of Evidence on the Intensive and Extensive Margins." *American Economic Review: Papers & Proceedings*, 101(3), 2011, pp. 471-475. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.3.471)
        - *Reconciles the apparent contradiction between small micro elasticities and larger macro elasticities by distinguishing intensive and extensive margins and accounting for frictions.*

    - **Keane, Michael P.** "Labor Supply and Taxes: A Survey." *Journal of Economic Literature*, 49(4), 2011, pp. 961-1075. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.49.4.961)
        - *Comprehensive review of how taxes affect labour supply, covering both structural and quasi-experimental evidence.*

!!! empirical "The Earned Income Tax Credit and Labour Supply"

    - **Eissa, Nada and Jeffrey B. Liebman.** "Labor Supply Response to the Earned Income Tax Credit." *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 111(2), 1996, pp. 605-637. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2946689)
        - *Seminal paper showing that the 1986 EITC expansion significantly increased labour force participation among single mothers -- the key extensive margin result.*

    - **Meyer, Bruce D. and Dan T. Rosenbaum.** "Welfare, the Earned Income Tax Credit, and the Labor Supply of Single Mothers." *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 116(3), 2001, pp. 1063-1114. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/00335530152466313)
        - *Extends the Eissa-Liebman analysis, disentangling the effects of welfare reform and EITC expansions on single mothers' labour supply.*

!!! empirical "Income Effects and Non-Labour Income"

    - **Imbens, Guido W., Donald B. Rubin, and Bruce I. Sacerdote.** "Estimating the Effect of Unearned Income on Labor Earnings, Savings, and Consumption: Evidence from a Survey of Lottery Players." *American Economic Review*, 91(4), 2001, pp. 778-794. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.91.4.778)
        - *Uses lottery winnings as exogenous variation in non-labour income to estimate the income effect. Finds that an additional dollar of non-labour income reduces labour earnings by about 11 cents -- confirming that leisure is a normal good.*

## Indian Context

!!! indian "Female LFPR in India -- The U-Shaped Debate"

    - **Goldin, Claudia.** "The U-Shaped Female Labor Force Function in Economic Development and Economic History." In *Investment in Women's Human Capital and Economic Development*, edited by T.P. Schultz, University of Chicago Press, 1995, pp. 61-90. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3386/w4707)
        - *The original statement of the U-shaped hypothesis: female LFPR first falls, then rises with economic development. The theoretical framework combines income effects, substitution effects, and social norms.*

    - **Klasen, Stephan and Janneke Pieters.** "What Explains the Stagnation of Female Labor Force Participation in Urban India?" *World Bank Economic Review*, 29(3), 2015, pp. 449-478. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/lhv003)
        - *Decomposes the decline in urban female LFPR using NSS data, finding that income growth (income effect), rising education (enrolment effect), and lack of suitable jobs all contributed.*

    - **Afridi, Farzana, Taryn Dinkelman, and Kanika Mahajan.** "Why Are Fewer Married Women Joining the Work Force in Rural India? A Decomposition Analysis over Two Decades." *Journal of Population Economics*, 31(3), 2018, pp. 783-818. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00148-017-0671-y)
        - *Uses NSS rounds to decompose the decline in rural married women's LFPR, highlighting the roles of income growth, education, and structural change away from agriculture.*

    - **Chatterjee, Esha, Sonalde Desai, and Reeve Vanneman.** "Indian Paradox: Rising Education, Declining Women's Employment." *Demographic Research*, 38, 2018, pp. 855-878. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4054/DemRes.2018.38.31)
        - *Documents the puzzle that rising female education in India has not translated into rising LFPR, and offers explanations related to job quality and social norms.*

!!! indian "NREGA and Rural Labour Supply"

    - **Imbert, Clement and John Papp.** "Labor Market Effects of Social Programs: Evidence from India's Employment Guarantee." *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 7(2), 2015, pp. 233-263. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20130401)
        - *Uses the phased rollout of NREGA as a natural experiment, finding significant increases in private-sector wages (4-5%) and a reallocation of labour from private to public employment.*

    - **Azam, Mehtabul.** "The Impact of Indian Job Guarantee Scheme on Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from a Natural Experiment." *IZA Discussion Paper No. 6548*, 2012.
        - *Finds positive effects of NREGA on female LFPR and wages, particularly during the agricultural lean season.*

    - **Muralidharan, Karthik, Paul Niehaus, and Sandip Sukhtankar.** "General Equilibrium Effects of (Improving) Public Employment Programs: Experimental Evidence from India." *NBER Working Paper No. 23838*, 2016.
        - *Randomised evaluation of Smartcard-based NREGA reforms in Andhra Pradesh, finding that improving NREGA implementation raised private-sector wages and incomes for the poorest households.*

## Stata Exercises

!!! stata "Basic Labour Supply Tabulations with PLFS Data"

    The following exercises use a person-level PLFS dataset. Adjust variable names to match your data extract.

    **Exercise 1: LFPR by gender and age group**

    ```stata
    * Create age groups
    gen agegroup = .
    replace agegroup = 1 if inrange(age, 15, 24)
    replace agegroup = 2 if inrange(age, 25, 34)
    replace agegroup = 3 if inrange(age, 35, 44)
    replace agegroup = 4 if inrange(age, 45, 54)
    replace agegroup = 5 if inrange(age, 55, 64)
    replace agegroup = 6 if age >= 65 & age < .

    label define agegrp 1 "15-24" 2 "25-34" 3 "35-44" ///
                        4 "45-54" 5 "55-64" 6 "65+"
    label values agegroup agegrp

    * Generate labour force indicator
    gen in_lf = inrange(usual_status, 11, 81)

    * LFPR by gender and age group
    tab agegroup in_lf [aw=weight] if sex == 1, row nofreq  // Males
    tab agegroup in_lf [aw=weight] if sex == 2, row nofreq  // Females
    ```

    **Exercise 2: Female LFPR by education and sector (urban/rural)**

    ```stata
    * Education groups (adjust codes to your dataset)
    gen edugroup = .
    replace edugroup = 1 if education <= 2   // Illiterate / below primary
    replace edugroup = 2 if education == 3   // Primary
    replace edugroup = 3 if education == 4   // Middle
    replace edugroup = 4 if education == 5   // Secondary
    replace edugroup = 5 if education == 6   // Higher secondary
    replace edugroup = 6 if education >= 7   // Graduate and above

    label define edgrp 1 "Below primary" 2 "Primary" 3 "Middle" ///
                       4 "Secondary" 5 "Higher sec." 6 "Graduate+"
    label values edugroup edgrp

    * Female LFPR by education, separately for rural and urban
    tab edugroup in_lf [aw=weight] if sex == 2 & sector == 1, row nofreq  // Rural
    tab edugroup in_lf [aw=weight] if sex == 2 & sector == 2, row nofreq  // Urban
    ```

    **Exercise 3: Hours worked distribution**

    ```stata
    * For employed workers, examine hours worked in the reference week
    * (current weekly status)
    histogram hours_worked if in_lf == 1 & hours_worked > 0 & sex == 1, ///
        width(5) frequency title("Weekly Hours: Males") ///
        xtitle("Hours worked per week") color(navy%60)

    histogram hours_worked if in_lf == 1 & hours_worked > 0 & sex == 2, ///
        width(5) frequency title("Weekly Hours: Females") ///
        xtitle("Hours worked per week") color(cranberry%60)

    * Summary statistics by gender
    tabstat hours_worked if hours_worked > 0, by(sex) stat(mean sd p25 p50 p75 n)
    ```

    **What to look for:** The male hours distribution should be concentrated around 48-56 hours/week. The female distribution should be more dispersed, with a significant mass at low hours (reflecting part-time and seasonal work) and a secondary peak around 35-42 hours (formal sector workers).

## Glossary Terms Used in This Chapter

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Budget constraint** | The set of consumption-leisure bundles affordable to the worker: \( C = w(T-L) + V \) |
| **Full income** | The maximum income a worker could earn if they worked all available hours, plus non-labour income: \( wT + V \) |
| **Indifference curve** | A curve showing all combinations of consumption and leisure that yield the same level of utility |
| **Marginal rate of substitution (MRS)** | The rate at which a worker is willing to trade leisure for consumption while remaining equally well off: \( MU_L / MU_C \) |
| **Reservation wage** | The minimum wage at which a non-participant would enter the labour force; equals the MRS at the endowment point |
| **Interior solution** | An optimum where the worker works positive hours (tangency condition: \( MRS = w \)) |
| **Corner solution** | An optimum where the worker does not work at all (\( L = T \)); occurs when \( w < w^* \) |
| **Income effect** | The change in hours worked due to the change in purchasing power from a wage change, holding relative prices constant; reduces hours if leisure is normal |
| **Substitution effect** | The change in hours worked due to the change in the relative price of leisure, holding utility constant; always increases hours when the wage rises |
| **Backward-bending supply curve** | A labour supply curve that bends backward at high wages, where the income effect dominates the substitution effect |
| **Added worker effect** | The tendency of secondary household members to enter the labour force when the primary earner loses their job |
| **Discouraged worker effect** | The tendency of workers to drop out of the labour force during recessions because they believe no suitable jobs are available |
| **Labour supply elasticity** | The percentage change in hours worked divided by the percentage change in the wage rate: \( \varepsilon = (\% \Delta h) / (\% \Delta w) \) |
| **Negative income tax (NIT)** | A transfer program providing a guaranteed income that is reduced at a specified rate as earnings rise |
| **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)** | A refundable tax credit that subsidises earnings in a phase-in range, then phases out at higher incomes |
| **Extensive margin** | The decision of whether to participate in the labour force (work vs. not work) |
| **Intensive margin** | The decision of how many hours to work, conditional on participation |
| **Non-labour income** | Income not derived from current work: dividends, interest, transfers, spouse's earnings, rental income |

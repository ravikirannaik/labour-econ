## Key Academic Papers

### Foundations of Search Theory

- **Stigler, George J.** "The Economics of Information." *Journal of Political Economy* 69(3): 213--225, 1961. [DOI: 10.1086/258464](https://doi.org/10.1086/258464)
    - The pioneering paper on information costs in markets. Stigler showed that price (and wage) dispersion persists because search is costly, and rational agents adopt optimal stopping rules rather than canvassing all sellers.

- **Stigler, George J.** "Information in the Labor Market." *Journal of Political Economy* 70(5, Part 2): 94--105, 1962. [DOI: 10.1086/258727](https://doi.org/10.1086/258727)
    - Extends the information economics framework to the labour market. Formalises why workers search for wages, how search intensity varies with costs and expected returns, and why wage dispersion persists in equilibrium.

- **Mortensen, Dale T.** "Job Search and Labor Market Analysis." In *Handbook of Labor Economics*, Vol. 2, edited by O. Ashenfelter and R. Layard, 849--919, 1986. [DOI: 10.1016/S1573-4463(86)02005-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1573-4463(86)02005-9)
    - Definitive survey of job search theory covering the reservation wage model, optimal search intensity, on-the-job search, and equilibrium search models. The standard reference for search theory in labour economics.

### Search and Matching Models

- **Diamond, Peter A.** "Aggregate Demand Management in Search Equilibrium." *Journal of Political Economy* 90(5): 881--894, 1982. [DOI: 10.1086/261099](https://doi.org/10.1086/261099)
    - Introduces the coconut model of search equilibrium, demonstrating that search frictions create trading externalities and multiple equilibria. Showed that aggregate demand management has real effects even in flexible-price economies with search frictions. (Nobel Prize in Economics, 2010.)

- **Mortensen, Dale T., and Christopher A. Pissarides.** "Job Creation and Job Destruction in the Theory of Unemployment." *Review of Economic Studies* 61(3): 397--415, 1994. [DOI: 10.2307/2297896](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297896)
    - The foundational model of equilibrium unemployment with endogenous job creation and destruction. The Mortensen-Pissarides framework became the workhorse model in macrolabour economics. (Nobel Prize in Economics, 2010, with Diamond.)

- **Shimer, Robert.** "The Cyclical Behavior of Equilibrium Unemployment and Vacancies." *American Economic Review* 95(1): 25--49, 2005. [DOI: 10.1257/0002828053828572](https://doi.org/10.1257/0002828053828572)
    - Shows that the standard Mortensen-Pissarides model cannot replicate the observed volatility of unemployment and vacancies over the business cycle --- the "Shimer puzzle." Productivity shocks alone generate far too little variation in labour market tightness.

- **Rogerson, Richard, Robert Shimer, and Randall Wright.** "Search-Theoretic Models of the Labor Market: A Survey." *Journal of Economic Literature* 43(4): 959--988, 2005. [DOI: 10.1257/002205105775362014](https://doi.org/10.1257/002205105775362014)
    - Comprehensive survey of search and matching models, covering wage determination, efficiency, directed vs. random search, and policy applications.

### Unemployment Insurance

- **Baily, Martin Neil.** "Some Aspects of Optimal Unemployment Insurance." *Journal of Public Economics* 10(3): 379--402, 1978. [DOI: 10.1016/0047-2727(78)90053-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727(78)90053-1)
    - Derives the optimal UI replacement rate formula, balancing insurance value against moral hazard. The Baily-Chetty formula remains the benchmark in the optimal UI literature.

- **Chetty, Raj.** "Moral Hazard versus Liquidity and Optimal Unemployment Insurance." *Journal of Political Economy* 116(2): 173--234, 2008. [DOI: 10.1086/588585](https://doi.org/10.1086/588585)
    - Separates the moral hazard and liquidity effects of UI. Finds that about 60% of the increase in unemployment duration from higher UI reflects the liquidity effect (consumption smoothing for cash-constrained workers) rather than moral hazard (reduced search effort). A landmark contribution to optimal UI design.

- **Card, David, Raj Chetty, and Andrea Weber.** "Cash-on-Hand and Competing Models of Intertemporal Behavior: New Evidence from the Labor Market." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 122(4): 1511--1560, 2007. [DOI: 10.1162/qjec.2007.122.4.1511](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2007.122.4.1511)
    - Exploits a discontinuity in Austrian severance pay to estimate the liquidity effect of cash-on-hand on unemployment duration. Workers with more liquidity search longer, consistent with the liquidity interpretation of UI effects.

- **Card, David, Jochen Kluve, and Andrea Weber.** "What Works? A Meta Analysis of Recent Active Labor Market Program Evaluations." *Journal of the European Economic Association* 16(3): 894--931, 2018. [DOI: 10.1093/jeea/jvx028](https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvx028)
    - Meta-analysis of 207 active labour market programme evaluations across countries. Finds that job search assistance is the most cost-effective intervention; subsidised public employment has limited long-term effects; training programmes show delayed but positive impacts.

### Duration Dependence and Stigma

- **Kroft, Kory, Fabian Lange, and Matthew J. Notowidigdo.** "Duration Dependence and Labor Market Conditions: Evidence from a Field Experiment." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 128(3): 1123--1167, 2013. [DOI: 10.1093/qje/qjt015](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjt015)
    - Correspondence study showing that employers discriminate against applicants with longer unemployment spells. Callback rates decline sharply with reported duration. The effect is stronger in tight labour markets, suggesting employer statistical discrimination rather than true skill depreciation.

### Phillips Curve and NAIRU

- **Friedman, Milton.** "The Role of Monetary Policy." *American Economic Review* 58(1): 1--17, 1968.
    - Presidential address introducing the natural rate hypothesis and the expectations-augmented Phillips curve. Argues that the long-run Phillips curve is vertical and that attempts to exploit the short-run trade-off lead to accelerating inflation. One of the most influential papers in macroeconomics.

- **Phillips, A. W.** "The Relation Between Unemployment and the Rate of Change of Money Wage Rates in the United Kingdom, 1861--1957." *Economica* 25(100): 283--299, 1958. [DOI: 10.1111/j.1468-0335.1958.tb00003.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1958.tb00003.x)
    - The original empirical documentation of the inverse relationship between unemployment and nominal wage growth, using nearly a century of UK data.

## Indian Context

### Unemployment Measurement and Trends

- **Mehrotra, Santosh, and Jajati K. Parida.** "India's Employment Crisis: Rising Education Levels and Falling Non-agricultural Job Growth." CSE Working Paper 2019-04, Centre for Sustainable Employment, Azim Premji University, 2019.
    - Analyses the paradox of rising educational attainment coinciding with stagnant non-agricultural employment growth. Documents the structural roots of India's educated unemployment problem and the failure of manufacturing to absorb surplus labour from agriculture.

- **Thomas, Jayan Jose.** "India's Labour Market during the 2000s: Surveying the Changes." *Economic and Political Weekly* 47(51): 39--51, 2012.
    - Comprehensive analysis of structural changes in India's labour market during the high-growth 2000s decade. Documents the disconnect between GDP growth and employment generation, the shift from self-employment to casual labour, and growing regional disparities.

- **Himanshu.** "Employment Trends in India: A Re-examination." *Economic and Political Weekly* 46(37): 43--59, 2011.
    - Critical reassessment of employment trends using NSS data. Highlights the inadequacy of standard unemployment measures for capturing labour market distress in India and the importance of examining underemployment and quality of employment alongside headline rates.

- **International Labour Organization (ILO).** *India Employment Report 2024: Youth Employment, Education and Skills.* ILO and Institute of Human Development, 2024.
    - Comprehensive report documenting India's youth employment challenge. Key findings: youth (15--29) account for 83% of the unemployed; unemployment rate among educated youth (secondary and above) was approximately 18.4%; the share of youth not in employment, education or training (NEET) remains high, especially for women. Available at [https://www.ilo.org/publications/india-employment-report-2024](https://www.ilo.org/publications/india-employment-report-2024).

- **Abraham, Vinoj.** "Employment Growth in Rural India: Distress-Driven?" *Economic and Political Weekly* 44(16): 97--104, 2009.
    - Distinguishes between productive employment and distress-driven self-employment in rural India. Argues that much of the apparent employment growth in rural areas reflects low-productivity survival activities rather than genuine economic opportunity.

### MGNREGA

- **Imbert, Clement, and John Papp.** "Labor Market Effects of Social Programs: Evidence from India's Employment Guarantee." *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* 7(2): 233--263, 2015. [DOI: 10.1257/app.20130401](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20130401)
    - Estimates that MGNREGA increased private-sector wages by 4.7% and reduced private-sector employment, confirming the programme's role as an outside option that raises the reservation wage in rural labour markets.

- **Zimmermann, Laura.** "Why Guarantee Employment? Evidence from a Large Indian Public-Works Program." Working Paper, University of Georgia, 2020.
    - Examines MGNREGA's effectiveness as insurance against income shocks. Finds evidence of consumption smoothing benefits, particularly for households in drought-prone districts.

- **Dreze, Jean, and Reetika Khera.** "The Battle for Employment Guarantee." *Frontline* 26(1), 2009.
    - Accessible overview of MGNREGA's design, implementation challenges, and political economy. Discusses wage payment delays, corruption, and the tension between employment guarantee and fiscal constraints.

### CMIE Data

- **Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy (CMIE).** Consumer Pyramids Household Survey (CPHS). Various waves, 2014--2023.
    - High-frequency panel data providing alternative unemployment estimates for India. CMIE often reports higher unemployment rates than PLFS, partly due to methodological differences in measuring employment status and a more frequent survey cycle that captures short-duration spells.

## Data Sources

| Source | Coverage | Key Variables |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| **PLFS** (Periodic Labour Force Survey) | Annual + quarterly, all-India | Unemployment (UPSS, CWS, CDS), age, education, state, industry |
| **CMIE-CPHS** (Consumer Pyramids) | Three waves/year, panel | Employment status, income, consumption, high-frequency dynamics |
| **BLS/CPS** (US Bureau of Labor Statistics) | Monthly, US | Unemployment rate (U-1 to U-6), duration, demographic breakdowns |
| **JOLTS** (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) | Monthly, US | Hires, separations, quits, layoffs, vacancies --- for flow analysis |
| **OECD Labour Force Statistics** | Annual/quarterly, 38 countries | Cross-country unemployment, duration, active labour market policy spending |
| **Eurostat** | Monthly/quarterly, EU | Unemployment by duration, youth unemployment, vacancy statistics |

## Stata Exercises

!!! stata "Calculating Unemployment Rates from PLFS"
    ```stata
    * Load PLFS person-level data
    use plfs_person_2024, clear

    * Define unemployment under CWS
    * Activity status codes: 81-82 = seeking/available for work
    * Codes 11-72 = various forms of employment
    gen unemployed_cws = (cws_status >= 81 & cws_status <= 82)
    gen in_lf_cws = (cws_status >= 11 & cws_status <= 82)

    * Overall CWS unemployment rate (15+ population)
    sum unemployed_cws if in_lf_cws == 1 & age >= 15 [aw = weight]

    * Unemployment rate by age group
    gen age_group = .
    replace age_group = 1 if age >= 15 & age <= 29
    replace age_group = 2 if age >= 30 & age <= 44
    replace age_group = 3 if age >= 45 & age <= 59
    label define agegrp 1 "Youth (15-29)" 2 "Prime (30-44)" 3 "Older (45-59)"
    label values age_group agegrp

    table age_group if in_lf_cws == 1 [aw = weight], ///
        stat(mean unemployed_cws) nformat(%9.3f)

    * Unemployment by education level
    table education_level if in_lf_cws == 1 [aw = weight], ///
        stat(mean unemployed_cws) nformat(%9.3f)

    * Verify India's educated unemployment paradox:
    * Graduate+ rate should exceed below-secondary rate
    ```

!!! stata "Steady-State Unemployment Simulation"
    ```stata
    * Simulate labour market flows to verify the steady-state formula
    * u* = s / (s + f)
    clear
    set obs 100  // 100 time periods

    gen t = _n
    scalar s = 0.02  // separation rate (2% per month)
    scalar f = 0.20  // finding rate (20% per month)
    scalar L = 1000000  // labour force

    * Analytical steady-state prediction
    scalar u_star = s / (s + f)
    display "Predicted steady-state rate: " u_star

    * Simulate dynamics starting from full employment (U=0)
    gen U = 0 in 1
    gen E = L in 1

    forvalues i = 2/100 {
        quietly replace U = U[`=`i'-1'] + s * E[`=`i'-1'] ///
            - f * U[`=`i'-1'] in `i'
        quietly replace E = L - U in `i'
    }

    gen u_rate = U / L
    line u_rate t, yline(`=u_star') ///
        title("Convergence to Steady-State Unemployment Rate") ///
        ytitle("Unemployment Rate") xtitle("Period (months)") ///
        note("Horizontal line = analytical prediction s/(s+f)")

    * Experiment: what if s doubles during periods 50-70 (recession)?
    * Re-run the simulation with time-varying s to see dynamics
    ```

!!! stata "Unemployment Duration Analysis (Survival Methods)"
    ```stata
    * Analyse unemployment duration determinants
    * Requires spell-level data with duration and exit indicator
    use unemployment_spells, clear

    * Declare survival-time data
    stset duration, failure(found_job)

    * Kaplan-Meier survival curve: proportion still unemployed
    sts graph, title("Unemployment Survival Curve") ///
        ytitle("Proportion Still Unemployed") xtitle("Weeks") ///
        risktable

    * Kaplan-Meier by UI receipt status
    sts graph, by(ui_recipient) ///
        title("Survival by UI Status") ///
        legend(order(1 "No UI" 2 "UI Recipient"))

    * Cox proportional hazard model
    * Positive coefs = faster exit (shorter spells)
    * Negative coefs = slower exit (longer spells)
    stcox age education female ui_recipient region_urban

    * Test for duration dependence using Weibull model
    streg age education female ui_recipient, dist(weibull)
    * Shape parameter p: if p < 1 → negative duration dependence
    *                     if p > 1 → positive duration dependence
    *                     if p = 1 → exponential (no dependence)
    ```

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Beveridge curve** | The downward-sloping relationship between the vacancy rate and the unemployment rate; outward shifts indicate deteriorating matching efficiency |
| **Cyclical unemployment** | Unemployment caused by downturns in the business cycle; temporary and responsive to macroeconomic stabilisation policy |
| **Discouraged workers** | People who want work but have stopped searching because they believe no jobs are available; excluded from the official unemployment count |
| **Duration dependence** | The phenomenon whereby the probability of exiting unemployment changes (usually falls) with the length of the unemployment spell |
| **Frictional unemployment** | Short-term unemployment arising from the normal process of matching workers with jobs; includes new entrants, re-entrants, and job-to-job transitions |
| **Job finding rate (\( f \))** | The probability that an unemployed worker finds and accepts a job in a given period |
| **Job separation rate (\( s \))** | The probability that an employed worker loses or leaves a job in a given period |
| **Liquidity effect (of UI)** | The component of the UI-duration relationship that reflects cash-constrained workers being enabled to search optimally, rather than accepting bad matches out of desperation |
| **Matching function** | \( M = A \cdot U^\alpha V^{1-\alpha} \); relates the number of new hires to the stocks of unemployment and vacancies, with \( A \) capturing matching efficiency |
| **MGNREGA** | Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (2005); guarantees 100 days of unskilled manual work per year to every rural household in India |
| **Moral hazard (of UI)** | The behavioural response to UI: workers reduce search intensity or raise reservation wages because unemployment is less costly, extending duration |
| **NAIRU** | Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment; the unemployment rate at which inflation neither accelerates nor decelerates; equivalent to the natural rate in the Friedman-Phelps framework |
| **Natural rate of unemployment** | The unemployment rate prevailing when cyclical unemployment is zero; equals frictional plus structural unemployment |
| **Phillips curve** | The inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation (or wage growth); vertical in the long run at the NAIRU |
| **Replacement rate** | The ratio of UI benefits to prior wages; a key parameter in UI design, typically 40--70% in OECD countries |
| **Reservation wage (\( w^R \))** | The lowest wage offer a worker is willing to accept; the optimal cutoff in a sequential search problem |
| **Steady-state unemployment rate** | \( u^* = s/(s+f) \); the unemployment rate at which flows into unemployment equal flows out |
| **Structural unemployment** | Unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers' skills or locations and the requirements or locations of available jobs; typically longer-lasting than frictional unemployment |
| **Underemployment** | A situation where workers are employed but at fewer hours, lower skill levels, or lower productivity than they desire or are capable of |
| **Wait unemployment** | Voluntary unemployment by educated workers who remain jobless while queuing for preferred formal-sector jobs rather than accepting available informal-sector work |

Keynes’s Prophecy


Why do we still work 40 hours a week?

Keynes’s 15-Hour Workweek

  • In 1930, Keynes predicted a 15-hour workweek by the end of the 20th century

  • Automation has accelerated, replacing human tasks with machines and robots

  • The average workweek remains largely unchanged, contrary to Keynes’s forecast

  • Ongoing automation trends indicate technology alone hasn’t significantly reduced working hours

In 1930, John Maynard Keynes penned one of the most audacious predictions in the history of economic thought. Writing in his essay “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren,” he forecast that by the end of the twentieth century, the average workweek would shrink to approximately fifteen hours. His reasoning followed straightforward compound growth logic: productivity had been growing at roughly two percent per year, implying a fourfold multiplication over seventy years. Maintaining 1930 living standards would thus require only a quarter of the labour input. He was approximately correct about productivity growth but spectacularly wrong about working hours — the workweek declined only from about 48 to about 40 hours.

Why Was Keynes Wrong?

  • Keynes accurately predicted economic growth but overlooked distribution and inequality

  • He miscalculated people’s willingness to stop working and underestimated the non-pecuniary rewards of work

  • The concept of “economic bliss” may have been influenced by the lifestyles of English gentlemen or the ideals of the Bloomsbury group

  • The debate encompasses crucial economic aspects: growth, inequality, wealth distribution, work, leisure, culture, and consumerism

The first and most significant reason for Keynes’s error concerns distribution. Productivity gains accrued disproportionately to capital owners and highly skilled workers, leaving many on a treadmill of long hours. The second reason involves revealed preferences for work — employment provides social identity, structured time, and a sense of purpose that pure leisure cannot replicate. Hedonic adaptation provides a third explanation: as incomes rose, so did consumption aspirations, making relative consumption more important than absolute levels. Finally, Keynes’s Bloomsbury circle placed exceptional value on leisure and aesthetic pursuits, which he may have unconsciously projected onto the general population. This essay reminds us that economic prediction requires understanding not just technology but also institutions, culture, preferences, and power dynamics — precisely the territory of labour economics.


Why Study Labour Economics?


Labour markets shape every dimension of adult life.

Why Should I Take Labour Econ?

  • Most of us will spend 30–40 years of our life working for an income

  • Labour markets help determine our:

    • Wealth and the goods we can afford
    • Who we associate with and the vacations we take
    • Which schools we (and our children) attend
    • Maybe, even who we will marry

Most students entering a course in labour economics have not fully considered how profoundly labour markets will shape every aspect of their adult lives. Starting salary differences compound dramatically over a career. The neighbourhood where one lives, the schools one’s children attend, the quality of healthcare one can access, and the vacations one can afford are all fundamentally constrained by labour market outcomes. The social networks people develop through careers often become their primary adult friendships. Geographic location is largely determined by labour markets, and occupational identity shapes self-concept in ways that persist even into retirement. Assuming 45 working years at 2,000 hours per year, that is 90,000 hours of work in a typical career — exceeding the time spent on any other single waking activity.

What Labour Economics Studies

  • Most of us will allocate a substantial fraction of our time to the labour market

  • Labour economics studies how labour markets work

  • Helps us understand and address many social and economic problems facing modern societies

Labour economics provides essential frameworks for addressing some of the most pressing questions facing contemporary societies. The field has evolved from a narrow focus on wages and employment to encompass virtually any economic decision involving time allocation, human capital investment, or the organization of work. This broad scope means that labour economics intersects with virtually every other field of economics and many areas of sociology, psychology, and public policy.

Questions in Labour Economics

  • Why did female labour force participation rise throughout the past century?

  • What is the impact of immigration on wages and employment of native-born workers?

  • Do minimum wages increase the unemployment rate of less-skilled workers?

  • Do wage and tax subsidies encourage firms to increase employment?

  • What is the impact of occupational safety and health regulation?

  • Are government subsidies of human capital investment effective?

  • Why did inequality rise more rapidly in the US in the 1980s?

The dramatic rise in female labour force participation in developed countries represents one of the most significant social transformations in human history — from about 20 percent in 1900 to over 60 percent by 2000 in the United States. India’s female LFPR stands at approximately 25 percent, among the lowest in the world. The immigration debate has become politically explosive: George Borjas argues immigration depresses wages for competing native workers, while David Card finds smaller effects. Minimum wage policy illustrates the value of empirical labour economics: Card and Krueger’s famous 1994 study of New Jersey’s minimum wage challenged decades of conventional wisdom by finding no negative employment effects in fast food restaurants. Without the tools of labour economics, these policy debates would consist entirely of ideological assertions rather than evidence-based reasoning.


The Evolution of Labour Economics


From description to causal inference.

Traditional Labour Economics

  • Descriptive: Documenting working conditions, union activity, institutional details

  • Less formal: Each situation seemed unique, requiring its own institutional knowledge

  • Typical micro-macro analysis without formal optimisation models

Labour economics has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past century, evolving from a largely descriptive enterprise to a rigorous analytical discipline. In the traditional approach before the mid-twentieth century, labour economists were primarily chroniclers and institutionalists. They described working conditions in mines and factories, documented the rise of trade unions, and studied the specifics of particular industries. The Wisconsin School, led by John Commons and Selig Perlman, exemplified this approach with its deep engagement with practical realities of industrial relations. This work was valuable — providing the empirical foundation for later theoretical developments — but offered limited capacity for prediction or generalization.

New Labour Economics / Personnel Economics

  • A new part of labour economics is personnel economics

  • In the past, there was no systematic discipline on which to base human resource questions

  • Always regarded as “too soft”

  • Today, we have models that can provide detailed and unambiguous answers

The new labour economics that emerged in the 1970s and 1980s, associated with the University of Chicago and scholars like Gary Becker and Jacob Mincer, brought formal optimisation models to the study of labour markets. Human capital theory provided a unified framework for understanding wage differentials, occupational choice, and lifecycle earnings patterns. Personnel economics, pioneered by Edward Lazear, applies economic reasoning to human resource questions within firms. For most of the twentieth century, decisions about hiring, compensation, and promotion were considered the province of HR management. Personnel economics challenges this view by demonstrating that economic models can provide detailed guidance on questions that previously seemed matters of intuition.

New Labour Economics: Key Questions

  • Are highly skilled people worth the additional salary cost?

  • How skilled is highly skilled? How should skill be defined?

  • Is formal education the key or should we use some other criterion?

  • What are the trade-offs between quantity and quality?

  • Are two unskilled workers more or less productive than one skilled worker?

These questions sit at the heart of personnel economics and reflect the kinds of decisions that firms face every day. The formal models of the new labour economics provide a framework for answering them rigorously — moving beyond intuition and organizational folklore to evidence-based decision-making. Understanding how to define, measure, and value skill is essential not just for firms but also for policy-makers designing education and training systems.

Labour Economics: A Timeline

  • Pre-1930s: Descriptive accounts of labour conditions; little formal theory

  • 1930s–40s: Institutionalists (Commons, Perlman); focus on unions and collective bargaining

  • 1970s–80s: Chicago School revival; human capital theory (Becker, Mincer); formal modelling

  • 1987–Present: Credibility revolution; causal inference methods (Card, Angrist); applied micro focus

The most recent transformation, the “credibility revolution,” began in the late 1980s and continues to the present day. Scholars like David Card and Joshua Angrist pioneered empirical methods — difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables — that provide more convincing evidence of causal relationships. Rather than simply documenting correlations, contemporary labour economists seek natural experiments and quasi-experimental variation to isolate causal effects. This methodological shift has not replaced theory — theory remains essential for knowing what questions to ask — but it has dramatically raised the evidentiary bar for empirical claims.


The Actors in the Labour Market


Workers, firms, and government.

Basics of the Labour Market

  • Participants are assigned motives:

  • Workers look for the “best” job

  • Firms look for profits

  • Government uses regulation to achieve goals of public policy:

    • Minimum wages
    • Occupational safety

The simple labour market model assigns motives to the three key actors. Workers seek to maximise their well-being — utility that depends not just on income but on leisure, working conditions, job security, and numerous other factors. Firms seek to maximise profits by hiring workers whose marginal product exceeds their wage. Government shapes the institutional framework within which exchange occurs, setting minimum wages, maximum hours, safety regulations, and anti-discrimination laws. These simplifications provide a powerful organising principle even though real behaviour is more complex.

Three “Actors” in Labour Markets

  • Workers: The most important actor — without workers, there is no “labour.” Desire to maximise well-being causes an upward-sloping labour supply curve.

  • Firms: Decide who to hire and fire. Motivated to maximise profits. The relation between \(P_L\) and \(Q_L\) yields the downward-sloping demand curve.

  • Government: Imposes taxes and regulations. Provides ground rules that guide exchanges in labour markets.

The worker’s problem involves allocating a fixed amount of time between market work and other activities including leisure and household production. Higher wages increase the opportunity cost of non-market time — the substitution effect — while also making workers richer, inducing demand for more leisure — the income effect. The balance of these two effects generates the upward-sloping supply curve. On the demand side, firms want workers because those workers produce output that can be sold for revenue. Labour demand is therefore a derived demand, depending on both the worker’s marginal physical product and the price at which output can be sold. The government sets the institutional framework: minimum wage floors, maximum hours, occupational safety standards, and anti-discrimination rules.


Why Do We Need a Theory?


“Observations always involve theory.”

— Edwin Hubble

Why Do We Need a Theory?

“Observations always involve theory.” — Edwin Hubble

  • Explain and understand how labour markets work

  • Focus on the essential variables while leaving out other, less crucial, factors

  • Create a model that helps explain the theory

The great astronomer Edwin Hubble once observed that “observations always involve theory.” Raw data about wages, employment, and working conditions are meaningless without a conceptual framework for interpretation. The unemployment rate can only be understood relative to a theory of what determines employment; wage differentials only make sense in the context of theories about productivity, discrimination, or compensating differentials. Economic models serve several essential purposes: they provide explanation by identifying causal mechanisms, they enable prediction about how outcomes change when circumstances change, and they impose analytical discipline by forcing explicit statement of assumptions.

What Makes a Good Model?

  • Clearly and simply explains a principle without extraneous detail

  • In economics, we generally use math (graphs)

  • Equations linking complex factors to study the effects of change

George Box, the statistician, famously declared that “all models are wrong, but some are useful.” The value of a model lies not in perfect correspondence to reality but in illuminating important aspects of complex phenomena. A model of labour supply that incorporates substitution and income effects does not merely describe how workers respond to wage changes; it explains why they respond as they do, tracing behaviour back to underlying preferences and constraints. The mathematical formulation ensures precision and prevents the ambiguity that can arise in purely verbal arguments.

Types of Models

  • Descriptive: Like our circular-flow diagram

  • Analytical:

    • Assumptions or axioms
    • Model — mathematical or logical
    • Conclusions deduced from the model

  • We test these, and if they work, we use them to describe the real world

Descriptive models organise facts and relationships without attempting formal deduction — the circular flow diagram showing households supplying labour and receiving wages is a canonical example. Analytical models go further: they start from explicit assumptions, build a mathematical or logical structure, and derive conclusions that can be confronted with data. If the predictions survive empirical testing, we gain confidence that the model captures something real about the underlying mechanisms. If they fail, we revise the model or its assumptions.

Problems with Models

  • The Assumptions:
    • Too many / too few
    • Are they correct?
  • The Math:
    • Can you really model the world with math?

Models face several common criticisms that students should understand. The assumptions underlying models are always unrealistic to some degree — real workers do not solve optimisation problems consciously, real firms do not calculate marginal products precisely, real markets do not clear instantly. The question is whether the simplifications are innocuous or whether they systematically bias conclusions. Determining this requires empirical investigation, not philosophical argument. Mathematics provides precision and prevents hidden assumptions, but it also imposes constraints on the kinds of phenomena that can be easily represented. The art of modelling lies in choosing the right level of abstraction.

How to Build an Economic Model

N. Gregory Mankiw’s advice:

  1. Look for ideas in the world, not in the journals
  2. First make your model as simple as possible, then generalise it
  3. Look at the literature later, not sooner
  4. Model your paper after your seminar
  5. Stop when you’ve made your point

Mankiw’s tips capture the art of economic modelling. The emphasis on simplicity first reflects the principle that a model should illuminate, not obscure. Looking at the literature later prevents being constrained by existing frameworks when fresh thinking might be valuable. Modelling the paper after a seminar ensures that the written version retains the clarity and persuasiveness of a good oral presentation. And stopping when the point has been made prevents the common academic vice of over-elaboration.


The Simple Labour Market Model


Supply, demand, and equilibrium.

Supply and Demand in the Engineering Labour Market

  • The labour supply curve gives the number of persons willing to supply their services at a given wage

  • The labour demand curve gives the number of engineers firms will hire at that wage

  • Equilibrium occurs where supply equals demand

The simple labour market model specifies supply and demand relationships and characterises their intersection as market equilibrium. The supply curve slopes upward because higher wages attract more workers — some switch from other markets, some enter the labour force, and some already in the market choose to work more hours. The demand curve slopes downward because higher wages increase the cost of labour relative to other inputs, inducing firms to economise on labour through substitution toward capital, reduced output, or exit. At the intersection, quantity supplied equals quantity demanded: workers who want jobs at that wage can find them, and firms that want to hire at that wage can find workers.

The Alaskan Pipeline: A Natural Experiment

  • Construction of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline shifted labour demand from \(D_0\) to \(D_1\)

  • Result: higher wages and employment in Alaska

  • Once the pipeline was completed, demand reverted and wages fell

The construction of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline System, beginning in 1974 following the oil crisis, provides a compelling illustration of the supply-demand model. The massive increase in construction activity represents a rightward shift in labour demand. With an unchanged supply curve, this demand shift predicts rising equilibrium wages and employment. The evidence confirms these predictions with remarkable clarity: both wages and employment in Alaska rose sharply as construction ramped up in the mid-1970s, peaked around 1976, and then declined as construction wound down in 1977. Alaska’s geographic isolation limited labour mobility, making the effects easier to identify. Source: Carrington (1996), Journal of Political Economy.

The Alaskan Pipeline: Wages and Employment

  • Both wages and employment rose sharply during pipeline construction (mid-1970s)

  • They declined as construction wound down — demand curve shifted back leftward

The figures show the time series of wages and employment in Alaska during the pipeline era. The sharp spike and subsequent decline illustrate the temporary nature of the demand shock. As construction wound down and the pipeline neared completion in 1977, wages and employment declined toward their pre-project levels. The demand curve shifted back leftward as temporary construction demand dissipated, and the market returned approximately to its original equilibrium. This case study is powerful precisely because the shock was large, temporary, and geographically contained — making it a near-ideal natural experiment for testing the basic supply-demand model.

Market Supply Function

  • Market supply for a particular industry: \(L^s = S(W, N, W^A)\)

  • \(W\) is an endogenous variable (determined within the model)

  • \(N\) and \(W^A\) are exogenous variables (determined outside the model)

  • Comparative statics:

    • Positive effect from wage \(W\)
    • Negative effect from alternative wages \(W^A\)
    • Positive effect from population size \(N\)

The labour supply function formalises the intuition that the quantity of labour supplied to a particular market depends on the wage offered, the size of the relevant population, and wages available in alternative employment. The distinction between endogenous and exogenous variables is fundamental to economic modelling. The wage is determined within the model by the interaction of supply and demand. Population and alternative wages are determined outside the model and taken as given. Comparative statics — comparing equilibrium outcomes before and after a parameter change — is the primary analytical method. Higher own-wages attract more workers, higher alternative wages draw them away, and a larger population increases the pool of potential workers.

Labour Demand: Derived Demand

  • Derived demand: Firms do not “want” labour — they want the output labour produces

  • Demand for workers depends on demand for the product itself — \(Q\)

  • We still need to pay the worker — \(W\)

  • We also need capital — \(R\)

  • Demand function: \(L^d = D(W, Q, R)\)

The crucial insight for understanding labour demand is that it represents a derived demand. Firms demand workers not for their own sake but for the output those workers produce. The value of a worker to a firm therefore depends fundamentally on two factors: the marginal physical product and the price at which output can be sold. If nobody wants to buy automobiles, automobile factories will not hire assembly workers no matter how cheaply they are willing to work. This is the channel through which macroeconomic conditions affect labour markets: recession reduces product demand, which reduces derived demand for labour, which generates unemployment.

Endogenous vs. Exogenous

  • Wages, labour demand, and labour supply are determined within the model. They are endogenous.

  • The demand for the product and the price of capital are outside the model. They are exogenous.

This figure illustrates the conceptual separation between endogenous and exogenous variables in the labour market model. Endogenous variables — wages, employment, labour supply and demand — are determined by the model itself through the interaction of supply and demand. Exogenous variables — product demand, capital costs, population, alternative wages — are determined outside the model and taken as given. This distinction is essential for proper causal interpretation. When product demand increases (exogenous shift), the model predicts how wages and employment adjust (endogenous response). Confusing endogenous with exogenous variables is the source of much confusion in policy debates.

Comparative Statics for Labour Demand

  • All else equal, an increase in wage \(W\) lowers the demand for labour

  • Demand for the product \(Q\) increases the demand for labour

  • The rental price of capital \(R\) may increase or decrease demand for labour:

    • Gross substitutes: Cheaper capital replaces workers
    • Gross complements: Cheaper capital enables expansion, increasing labour demand

Higher product demand increases labour demand because producing more output requires more workers. The effect of capital costs is theoretically ambiguous because two forces work in opposite directions. When capital becomes cheaper, firms might substitute machinery for workers, reducing labour demand — this is the substitution effect. Alternatively, cheaper capital might reduce production costs, potentially expanding output and thus increasing labour demand — this is the scale effect. Whether capital and labour are gross substitutes or gross complements depends on which force dominates. This distinction has become increasingly important in the age of artificial intelligence and automation.

Labour Market Equilibrium

  • The wage rate adjusts so that \(L^S = L^D = L\)

  • Both workers’ and employers’ plans are consistent

  • Surplus and shortage mechanisms drive adjustment:

    • Wage above equilibrium \(\rightarrow\) surplus of workers \(\rightarrow\) wage bid down
    • Wage below equilibrium \(\rightarrow\) shortage of workers \(\rightarrow\) wage bid up

Labour market equilibrium occurs at the wage where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. At this equilibrium wage, the plans of workers and firms are mutually consistent. Workers who wish to work at that wage can find employment; firms that wish to hire at that wage can find workers. No one has an incentive to change their behaviour. If the wage exceeds equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded — a surplus of workers exists and unemployed workers bid down the wage. If the wage falls below equilibrium, a shortage exists and firms bid up the wage. The market naturally gravitates toward equilibrium through these adjustment mechanisms.

Real-World Applications of Supply and Demand

  • The simple model explains real-world events:
    • Alyeska pipeline: positive demand shock \(\rightarrow\) temporary wage increase
    • Black Death (1346–1353): supply decrease \(\rightarrow\) wage increase
    • Palestinian intifada (1987): supply decrease in Israel \(\rightarrow\) Israeli wage increase
    • Dot-com bubble (2002): demand decrease \(\rightarrow\) wage decrease
    • COVID-19 pandemic: complex supply and demand shifts
  • Challenges: Difficult to further modify models to capture these complex phenomena

The same framework illuminates diverse historical episodes. The Black Death that swept through Europe between 1346 and 1353 killed perhaps one-third of the population — a massive leftward shift in labour supply. With fewer workers and relatively unchanged demand, the model predicts rising wages for survivors, and this is precisely what medieval chroniclers recorded. The Palestinian intifada restricted the flow of Palestinian workers into Israel, reducing labour supply and raising Israeli wages. The dot-com bust reduced demand for technology workers, lowering their wages. COVID-19 produced unusually complex shifts on both sides simultaneously — supply restrictions from lockdowns combined with demand shifts from changed consumption patterns. Each case demonstrates the model’s power and, in the COVID case, its limitations when shocks hit both sides of the market.


Beyond the Simple Model


Institutions, bounded rationality, and market failures.

New Institutional Economics (NIE)

  • Gary Becker and the family; Oliver Williamson and the firm (transaction costs)

  • NIE explains institutions in terms of actions and goals of individuals who participate in them

  • Uses bounded rationality and transaction costs theory

  • North (1990): “Institutions are the rules of the game in a society”

The simple supply-demand model rests on assumptions that merit scrutiny. Neoclassical economics assumes perfectly rational agents who can identify all options, evaluate consequences, and choose optimally. New Institutional Economics, pioneered by Becker on the family and Williamson on the firm, extends economic analysis while relaxing some of these restrictive assumptions. Neoclassical economics treats firms and households as “black boxes” characterised by production and utility functions but without attention to internal structure. NIE opens these boxes to examine how institutions — the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that structure human interaction — affect economic outcomes.

Bounded Rationality

  • Bounded rationality: rational choice that takes into account the cognitive limitations of the decision-maker

  • Limitations of both knowledge and computational capacity

  • Example: Buying “cage-free” eggs without researching whether “cage-free” means “free-run” (indoors) or “free-range” (outdoor access)

Herbert Simon coined the term “bounded rationality” and received the Nobel Prize in 1978 for his work on decision-making in organisations. Boundedly rational agents seek to maximise their objectives but face cognitive limitations. Real decision-makers have limited information about options available to them and limited computational capacity to process even the information they possess. They “satisfice” — identifying options that meet acceptable thresholds rather than searching exhaustively for the absolute best. The cage-free eggs example illustrates this perfectly: the consumer satisfies an ethical criterion (no cages) without optimising within that criterion (distinguishing between free-run and genuine free-range). This more realistic conception of human decision-making has profound implications for how we model labour market choices.


Efficiency and Welfare Economics


What is, what should be, and what is “best.”

Positive Versus Normative Economics

  • Positive economics:
    • Addresses the facts — focuses on “what is”
    • Questions answered with tools of economists
  • Normative economics:
    • Addresses values — focuses on “what should be”
    • Requires judgments

The philosopher David Hume argued that one cannot derive “ought” from “is” — facts alone do not imply values. Positive economics concerns facts and causal relationships: “How would a minimum wage increase affect employment?” is a positive question, answerable through empirical investigation. Normative economics concerns values: “Should we raise the minimum wage?” requires not just factual knowledge but value judgments about which consequences are desirable. Economists have no special expertise on value questions; their contribution lies in clarifying what consequences would follow from alternative policies, allowing society to make informed normative choices.

Pareto Efficiency

  • Our best attempt to eliminate the normative impact of what is “best”

  • An allocation is Pareto efficient IF there is no feasible alternative that can increase the well-being of at least one individual without hurting anyone else

  • Equivalently: all gains from trade have been exhausted

Named after the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto (1848–1923), this concept provides a criterion for evaluating resource allocations that avoids interpersonal utility comparisons. An allocation is Pareto efficient if there exists no feasible reallocation that would make at least one person better off without making anyone else worse off. The concept is elegant because it requires only ordinal preferences — we need to know whether someone prefers A to B, not by how much. However, as we will see, this elegance comes at the cost of limited applicability, since most real-world policy changes create both winners and losers.

Pareto Efficiency: The Rancho-Raju Example

  • Two consumers: Rancho and Raju; two goods: umbrellas and bread

  • Rancho: 5 umbrellas, 2 bread; MRS = 2 (gives 2 umbrellas for 1 bread)

  • Raju: 2 umbrellas, 6 bread; MRS = 1/3 (gives 3 bread for 1 umbrella)

  • Since \(MRS_{Rancho} \neq MRS_{Raju}\), gains from trade exist

  • Trade until \(MRS_{Rancho} = MRS_{Raju}\) — now Pareto efficient

This example illustrates the core logic of exchange efficiency. Because Rancho values bread relatively more than umbrellas (willing to give up 2 umbrellas per bread) while Raju values umbrellas relatively more (willing to give up 3 bread per umbrella), mutually beneficial trade is possible. Rancho could give 2 umbrellas to Raju in exchange for 1 loaf of bread; Rancho would be exactly as well off, while Raju would be strictly better off. Trade continues until marginal rates of substitution are equalised, at which point no further mutually beneficial exchange is possible and the allocation is Pareto efficient.

Three Aspects of Pareto Efficiency

  • Exchange/Allocative efficiency: Goods must go to those who value them most. No scope for mutually advantageous trade.

  • Production efficiency: One good’s increase should not decrease another’s. Firm operates on the production possibilities frontier.

  • Product mix efficiency: Produced goods should match preferences. \(MRT = MRS\).

Full Pareto efficiency requires three conditions to hold simultaneously. Exchange efficiency requires that goods be distributed so that no mutually beneficial trades remain — equalisation of marginal rates of substitution across consumers. Production efficiency requires that the economy operate on its production possibilities frontier — it should be impossible to produce more of one good without producing less of another. Product mix efficiency requires that the combination of goods actually produced corresponds to consumer preferences — the marginal rate of transformation in production must equal the marginal rate of substitution in consumption. Under perfect competition, all three conditions are satisfied simultaneously because both firms and consumers equate their marginal rates to the common price ratio.

The Fundamental Welfare Theorems

  • First theorem: Competitive markets (with certain conditions) lead to Pareto efficiency

  • Second theorem: By redistributing wealth, competitive markets can achieve any Pareto efficient allocation

  • Conditions for the First Welfare Theorem:

    • Perfect competition, complete markets, no externalities
    • Perfect information, no public goods, no transaction costs

The First Fundamental Theorem formalises Adam Smith’s insight about the “invisible hand” — individuals pursuing their own self-interest can, under appropriate institutional arrangements, produce socially efficient outcomes without conscious coordination. As Milton and Rose Friedman put it: “Adam Smith’s flash of genius was his recognition that the prices that emerged from voluntary transactions between buyers and sellers could coordinate the activity of millions of people, each seeking his own interest, in such a way as to make everyone better off.” The Second Theorem establishes that any Pareto efficient allocation can be achieved via competitive markets given appropriate redistribution. However, “only” an initial wealth redistribution is needed is easier said than done — low-cost enforcement of property rights, contracts, and competitive institutions is not easy. “Let markets handle it” is an illusion without hard work to create and maintain competitive institutions.

The “Weakness” of Pareto Efficiency

  • Pareto efficiency is a rather “weak” concept

  • Many Pareto efficient allocations exist:

    • An allocation where one agent gets all resources is Pareto efficient!
    • Any change that helps the poor would hurt the rich

  • Many allocations cannot be Pareto ranked

  • Pareto efficiency does not address distributional issues

Despite its elegance, Pareto efficiency has significant limitations. Most fundamentally, many allocations cannot be Pareto ranked relative to each other. If moving from allocation A to allocation B helps some people and hurts others, Pareto efficiency provides no guidance. Since virtually all real policy choices involve such tradeoffs, the Pareto criterion has limited direct applicability. The most striking illustration is that an allocation where one person has everything and everyone else has nothing is Pareto efficient — any change that helps the poor would hurt the rich. Pareto efficiency rules out only the most obvious forms of waste; it says nothing about fairness or justice.

Kaldor-Hicks Efficiency

  • Strict Pareto improvements are rare in actual policy — so we use Kaldor-Hicks (KH) efficiency

  • For a change to be KH efficient: those made better off could in principle compensate those made worse off

  • Unit of measurement is “wealth” or “price” — not utility

  • This introduces the notion of “ability to pay”

Because strict Pareto improvements are rare in actual policy choices, economists developed Kaldor-Hicks efficiency as an alternative criterion. Actual compensation is not required; only the possibility matters. This provides the theoretical foundation for cost-benefit analysis: a project is worthwhile if its total benefits exceed its total costs, even if beneficiaries and cost-bearers are different people. The bridge example illustrates the distinction: building a bridge creates tolerable benefits for users but imposes costs on nearby shopkeepers who lose traffic and neighbours who suffer noise. If total benefits exceed total costs, the project is KH efficient even without compensating the losers.

The Milk Problem: Limits of Wealth Maximisation

  • Two individuals — one rich and one poor — both desire a cup of milk

  • The poor person wants it desperately, willing to pay their last dollar ($1.00)

  • The rich person thinks it would be fun to pour the milk into a storm drain, willing to pay $1.50

  • Under wealth maximisation: the “efficient” allocation goes to the rich person

  • “Ability to pay” is an imperfect substitute for actual well-being

The Kaldor-Hicks criterion has attracted significant criticism because actual compensation is not required, making “ability to pay” rather than intensity of preference the measure of value. This milk example illustrates the problem starkly. The rich person who values something trivially is counted more than a poor person who values something desperately, simply because the rich person can pay more. This distinction between efficiency and equity is fundamental: efficiency means society gets the most from its scarce resources, while equity means those resources are distributed fairly. Labour economics constantly confronts this tension — policies that increase efficiency may worsen inequality, and vice versa.


Market Failures


When the invisible hand fumbles.

When Do Markets Fail?

  • Ignorance: smoker in an asbestos plant; not knowing about a job opening

  • Transaction barriers: laws restricting hours, costs of moving

  • Price distortions: taxes can create “incorrect” prices

  • Missing markets: impossible or not customary to transact

When the stringent conditions required by the First Welfare Theorem fail to hold, competitive markets may not achieve Pareto efficiency. Understanding these “market failures” is essential for labour economists, both because labour markets frequently exhibit them and because they provide the principal economic rationale for government intervention. Labour markets facilitate voluntary, mutually beneficial transactions, but they are particularly susceptible to information problems, transaction costs, and externalities that can drive outcomes far from the efficient benchmark.

Six Cases of Market Failure

  1. Failure of competition: Monopolies, oligopolies charge above marginal cost

  2. Externalities: Costs/benefits spill over to third parties

  3. Incomplete markets: Some markets simply don’t exist

  4. Information failures: Buyers/sellers lack full information (the “lemons” problem)

  5. Unemployment and disequilibrium: Markets don’t always clear

  6. Public goods: Non-excludable, non-rival goods are underprovided

Each of these failures violates one of the assumptions of the First Welfare Theorem. Failure of competition means firms can set prices above marginal cost. Externalities mean that some costs or benefits are not captured by market prices — a factory polluting a river imposes health costs on villagers. Incomplete markets mean that some valuable transactions cannot occur — no insurance for flood-prone slum dwellers. Information failures, as George Akerlof identified in the “lemons” problem, arise when sellers know more about quality than buyers. In labour markets, similar problems arise when workers know more about their abilities than employers. Unemployment represents perhaps the most visible market failure, where willing workers simply cannot find jobs during recessions. Public goods like street lighting are underprovided because everyone benefits but no one pays voluntarily.


Regression Basics


The empirical toolkit of labour economics.

What Is Regression Analysis?

  • A set of statistical processes for estimating relationships between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables

  • “To understand the causes of things”

  • Uses of regression: prediction, exploring associations, extrapolation, and causal inference

Regression analysis constitutes the primary statistical tool of modern labour economics. At its core, it provides a rigorous method for estimating how a dependent variable relates to one or more independent variables. Statistics has been called the universal language of scientific evidence — confidence intervals, p-values, and posterior distributions mean the same thing across every scientific discipline. In labour economics, regression is used for prediction (forecasting wages), exploring associations (identifying risk factors for unemployment), extrapolation (adjusting for sample-population differences), and most importantly, causal inference (estimating treatment effects of policies like minimum wage increases).

Wages and Schooling: Setup

  • There are sizable wage differences across occupations

  • One obvious factor: level of education of workers in that occupation

  • Suppose a linear equation: \(\log w = \alpha + \beta s\)

    • \(\log w\): average log wage (dependent variable)
    • \(s\): mean years of schooling (independent variable)
    • Objective: estimate \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) using actual data

The relationship between wages and education provides a classic application of regression in labour economics. We observe that wages vary substantially across occupations and hypothesise that education is a key determinant. Labour economists typically study log wages rather than wage levels because small changes in log wages approximate percentage changes. A coefficient of 0.10 on years of schooling means approximately 10 percent higher wages per additional year. The logarithmic specification also tends to produce better-behaved residuals, since the wage distribution is right-skewed but the log-wage distribution is approximately normal.

Scatter Diagram and Regression Line

  • The regression line is the line that best summarises the data

  • It minimises the sum of squared vertical distances between points and the line

The scatter diagram plots each occupation as a point with mean years of schooling on the horizontal axis and average log wage on the vertical axis. The positive slope is visually apparent — occupations with more educated workers tend to pay higher wages. Among the many lines that could be drawn through the data, the ordinary least squares regression line minimises the sum of squared vertical distances between observed points and predicted values. This criterion has desirable statistical properties: the resulting estimates are unbiased and have the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators under standard assumptions.

Estimated Regression

  • Applying the formula: \(\log w = 0.869 + 0.143s\)

  • The estimated slope is positive: average log wage is higher in occupations with more education

  • One-year increase in mean schooling \(\Rightarrow\) wage increases by approximately 14.3%

  • Intercept: log wage would be 0.869 if schooling = 0 (extrapolation warning!)

  • Caution: out-of-sample prediction can give absurd results

The estimated slope of 0.143 indicates that occupations where workers have one additional year of schooling on average have wages approximately 14.3 percent higher. The intercept of 0.869 would represent the log wage in an occupation requiring zero years of schooling — but since no such occupation exists in the data, this is an extrapolation that should be interpreted cautiously. Out-of-sample prediction can produce nonsensical results, which is why economists emphasise the range of the data when making predictions. The least squares method identifies this particular line by minimising the sum of squared differences between observed and predicted values.

Standard Errors and Statistical Significance

  • Standard errors measure statistical precision: \(SE(\beta) = 0.012\)

  • Margin of error \(\approx 2 \times SE\): the schooling effect is \(0.143 \pm 0.024\) (range: 11.9%–16.7%)

  • The t-statistic: \(t = \frac{|\text{Coefficient}|}{\text{Standard Error}} = \frac{0.143}{0.012} = 11.9\)

  • If \(t > 2\): coefficient is “significantly different from zero”

  • \(R^2 = 0.762\): schooling explains 76.2% of the variation in mean log wages across occupations

Statistical theory tells us the true impact lies within the range defined by the coefficient plus or minus twice its standard error with approximately 95 percent probability. The t-statistic of 11.9 far exceeds the critical value of 2, meaning we can confidently reject the hypothesis that education has no effect on wages. The R-squared of 0.762 indicates that differences in educational attainment explain over three-quarters of the variation in average wages across occupations — a remarkably strong relationship that helps explain why economists earn more than construction labourers. The remaining 24 percent is attributable to other factors such as working conditions, required experience, and compensating differentials.


Summary and Course Roadmap


Summary

  • Labour economics studies how labour markets work

  • Models typically contain three actors: workers, firms, and governments

  • A good theory should have realistic assumptions and be testable with real-world data

  • The tools of economics are helpful in answering positive questions

This chapter has introduced the fundamental building blocks of labour economics. We began with Keynes’s famous prediction about the 15-hour workweek to motivate why the field matters — understanding why that prediction failed requires exactly the tools labour economics provides. We examined the three actors in labour markets, the role of economic models, the supply-demand framework with the Alaskan pipeline as a case study, and the welfare economics concepts that underpin policy analysis. The key takeaway is that labour economics provides rigorous analytical tools for understanding the most important market most of us will ever participate in.

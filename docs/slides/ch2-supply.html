The Neoclassical Model of Labour-Leisure Choice


Neo-Classical Model of Labor-Leisure Choice

  • Utility Function — the worker derives satisfaction from consumption (\(C\)) and leisure (\(L\))

  • Indifference Curves — all combinations of \(C\) and \(L\) that yield the same utility

  • Budget Constraint — the set of affordable consumption-leisure bundles

  • The Hours of Work Decision — maximise utility subject to the budget constraint

  • Optimal Consumption and Leisure — tangency of indifference curve and budget line

We now set up the core neoclassical model of labour supply. The worker has preferences over two “goods” — consumption of market goods and leisure — and faces a constraint determined by the wage rate, non-labour income, and the total time endowment. The model generates predictions about how changes in wages and income affect the decision to work and how many hours to work. Every subsequent topic in this chapter — reservation wages, backward-bending supply curves, welfare programs, the EITC — is an application of this basic framework.

Indifference Curves

Points X and Y lie on the same indifference curve and yield the same utility (say, 25,000 utils). Point Z lies on a higher indifference curve and therefore yields more utility. The four key properties of indifference curves are: they are downward-sloping (reflecting the trade-off between consumption and leisure), higher curves represent higher utility, they do not intersect, and they are convex to the origin (reflecting diminishing marginal rate of substitution — as you have more leisure, each additional hour of leisure is worth less in consumption terms).

Indifference Curves Cannot Cross

This is a proof-by-contradiction diagram. Points X and Y yield the same utility because they lie on the same indifference curve. Points Y and Z should also yield the same utility because they too lie on a common indifference curve. By transitivity, X and Z should yield the same utility — but Z clearly has more of both goods than X, so Z must be preferred. This contradiction proves that indifference curves cannot intersect. The result depends on the assumption that preferences are transitive and that more is preferred to less.

Differences in Preferences Across Workers

Panel (a) shows Cindy’s indifference curves, which are steep — she requires a substantial bribe in the form of additional consumption to give up an hour of leisure. Cindy places a high value on her free time. Panel (b) shows Mindy’s indifference curves, which are much flatter — she attaches a lower value to leisure and is willing to trade it away for relatively small increases in consumption. These differences in preferences will lead Cindy and Mindy to make very different labour supply decisions even if they face identical wages and non-labour income. Preference heterogeneity is a key reason why we observe different people working very different hours at the same wage.

The Budget Constraint

  • The budget constraint defines the worker’s opportunity set — all consumption-leisure combinations the worker can afford.

\[C = wh + V\]

  • Consumption equals labour earnings (\(w \times\) hours of work) plus non-labour income (\(V\))

  • Since \(h = T - L\), we can rewrite:

\[C = w(T - L) + V\]

The budget constraint is the centrepiece of the model. Total consumption equals wage income plus non-labour income. Non-labour income includes things like investment returns, spousal earnings, or government transfers. The key insight is that the wage rate represents the price of leisure — every hour not worked costs the worker \(w\) in forgone earnings. The time endowment \(T\) is typically set at 24 hours per day or some fraction of waking hours. The constraint is linear in consumption-leisure space, with slope equal to the negative of the wage rate.

Graphing the Budget Constraint

Point E is the endowment point, showing the consumption the worker can afford if she does not work at all — this equals her non-labour income \(V\). As the worker trades leisure for work, she moves up and to the left along the budget line, gaining \(w\) dollars of consumption for each hour of leisure given up. The absolute value of the slope of the budget line is the wage rate \(w\). The horizontal intercept is the total time endowment \(T\) (maximum possible leisure), and the vertical intercept is \(wT + V\) (maximum possible consumption if all time is spent working).

The Hours of Work Decision

  • Individuals choose consumption and leisure to maximise utility

  • Optimal consumption is given by the point where the budget line is tangent to the indifference curve

    • At this point, the marginal rate of substitution (MRS) between consumption and leisure equals the wage
    • Any other consumption-leisure bundle on the budget constraint would give the individual less utility

The worker solves a constrained optimisation problem: maximise \(U(C, L)\) subject to \(C = w(T - L) + V\). The first-order condition requires that the marginal rate of substitution between consumption and leisure equals the wage rate. Intuitively, at the optimum, the rate at which the worker is willing to trade leisure for consumption (the MRS, given by the slope of the indifference curve) exactly equals the rate at which the market allows her to make that trade (the wage rate, given by the slope of the budget line). Interior solutions occur when the worker chooses to work positive hours; corner solutions arise when the worker prefers not to work at all.

Optimal Consumption and Leisure

A utility-maximising worker chooses the consumption-leisure bundle at point P, where the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line. At this point, the slopes are equal: \(MRS = w\). The worker cannot reach a higher indifference curve because it lies entirely above the budget constraint. Any other point on the budget line — say, to the left or right of P — would place the worker on a lower indifference curve. This is the fundamental diagram of labour supply theory, and every subsequent comparative static builds on this tangency condition.

Your Turn 1

Suppose that at a particular combination of income and leisure, the slope of the budget line is steeper than the slope of the indifference curve it intersects.

  1. How should the worker adjust work hours?

If the slope of the budget line is steeper than the slope of the indifference curve at their intersection, the market wage exceeds the worker’s marginal rate of substitution — meaning the market values an hour of work more highly than the worker values the corresponding hour of leisure. The worker should therefore work more hours, moving up and to the left along the budget line. This allows her to reach a higher indifference curve. She continues increasing hours until the MRS rises to equal the wage rate — that is, until the tangency condition is restored. This exercise reinforces that the optimum requires MRS = w, and any deviation from tangency implies the worker can improve her utility by adjusting hours.


Income and Substitution Effects


Effect of a Change in Non-Labour Income (Leisure Normal)

An increase in non-labour income leads to a parallel, upward shift in the budget line — the slope (wage rate) is unchanged but the endowment point moves up. If leisure is a normal good, the worker moves from point P0 to point P1, consuming more leisure and working fewer hours. This is a pure income effect: the worker is richer and “buys” more of everything, including leisure. The assumption that leisure is normal is standard and well-supported empirically — lottery winners and inheritance recipients tend to reduce their work hours.

Effect of a Change in Non-Labour Income (Leisure Inferior)

In the unusual case where leisure is an inferior good, an increase in non-labour income shifts the budget line up but the worker actually increases hours of work. The new tangency point P1 involves less leisure than P0. While this is theoretically possible, it is empirically uncommon. Most evidence supports the assumption that leisure is a normal good. The distinction matters because it determines the sign of the income effect in the Slutsky decomposition of wage changes.

Effect of a Change in the Wage Rate

A change in the wage rate rotates the budget line around the endowment point E. A higher wage makes the budget line steeper — each hour of work now buys more consumption. The worker moves from point P to point R. Whether hours of work increase or decrease depends on the relative magnitudes of the income and substitution effects. When the income effect dominates the substitution effect, the worker increases hours of leisure in response to a wage increase — she is sufficiently richer that she “buys” more leisure despite its higher opportunity cost.

Effect of a Change in the Wage Rate on Hours

This figure shows the alternative case where the substitution effect dominates. A wage increase makes leisure more expensive relative to consumption, and the worker responds by consuming less leisure and working more hours. The move from P to R involves an increase in hours worked. Whether the income or substitution effect dominates is ultimately an empirical question that differs across individuals and across the wage distribution.

Decomposing Income and Substitution Effects

An increase in the wage rate generates both income and substitution effects. The income effect (the move from point P to point Q, along the hypothetical parallel budget line tangent to the original indifference curve) reduces hours of work — the worker is richer and demands more leisure. The substitution effect (the move from Q to R, along the new, steeper budget line) increases hours of work — leisure is now more expensive. The net effect on hours depends on which force is stronger. This Slutsky decomposition is the analytical core of labour supply theory.

Effect of Wage Increase on Labor Supply

1. Substitution effect:

  • Working more lucrative
  • Leisure more “expensive”
  • Wage is opportunity cost of leisure
  • Households substitute towards lucrative activities and away from expensive activities
  • Work more

2. Income effect:

  • Higher wage means worker is richer
  • Richer workers choose more consumption and more leisure
  • Leisure is a “normal” good (you buy more of it the richer you are)
  • More leisure means less work
  • Work less

This side-by-side summary crystallises the two opposing forces. The substitution effect always pushes toward more work when wages rise — leisure has become more expensive relative to consumption. The income effect always pushes toward less work when wages rise — the worker is wealthier and wants more of all normal goods, including leisure. The empirical labour supply literature is essentially an attempt to measure which of these two effects dominates, for whom, and under what circumstances.

Wage Changes and Hours of Work: Summary Table

This summary table from Cahuc, Laing, and Ehrenberg neatly organises the predictions. When the substitution effect dominates, hours and wages move in the same direction. When the income effect dominates, they move in opposite directions. The table also distinguishes between changes in the wage rate (which generate both effects) and changes in non-labour income (which generate only an income effect). These predictions form the testable hypotheses that the empirical literature in Section 2-8 attempts to evaluate.


To Work or Not to Work?


To Work or Not to Work?

  • Are the “terms of trade” sufficiently attractive to “bribe” a worker to enter the labour market?

  • Reservation wage: The lowest wage rate that would make the person indifferent between working and not working.

    • Rule 1: If the market wage is less than the reservation wage, the person will not work.
    • Rule 2: The reservation wage increases as non-labour income increases.

The reservation wage is the critical concept linking the interior solution (positive hours worked) to the corner solution (zero hours worked). It equals the marginal rate of substitution evaluated at the endowment point — that is, how much consumption the worker demands in exchange for the very first hour of work. If the going market wage falls below this threshold, the worker stays out of the labour force entirely. The reservation wage depends on preferences (how much the person values leisure) and non-labour income (the richer you are, the higher the wage must be to tempt you into the labour market).

Labor Supply and Reservation Wage

The reservation wage (\(w^*\)) is the wage rate that makes the worker indifferent between working and not working. Graphically, it is given by the absolute value of the slope of the indifference curve at the endowment point E — the point where the worker consumes all her time as leisure and lives on non-labour income alone. At this point, the worker is evaluating whether the first hour of work is “worth it.”

Labor Supply and Reservation Wage (cont.)

The reservation wage can also be interpreted as the minimum income increase that would make the person indifferent between staying at the endowment point E and working the first hour. If the market wage equals the reservation wage, the budget line is just tangent to the indifference curve at E — the worker is on the margin of entering the labour force.

Labor Supply and Reservation Wage (cont.)

When the market wage exceeds the reservation wage, the budget line is steeper than the indifference curve at E, and the worker can reach a higher indifference curve by working positive hours. When the market wage is below the reservation wage, the budget line is flatter than the indifference curve at E, and the worker is better off not working at all. This clean binary prediction — work if \(w > w^*\), don’t work if \(w < w^*\) — is the foundation for understanding labour force participation decisions.


The Labor Supply Curve


Labor Supply Curve

  • Relationship between hours worked and the wage rate

  • At wages slightly above the reservation wage, the labor supply curve is positively sloped (the substitution effect dominates the income effect)

  • If the income effect begins to dominate the substitution effect, hours of work decline as the wage rate increases (a negatively sloped labor supply curve)

The individual labour supply curve maps out the optimal hours of work at every possible wage rate. At very low wages (just above the reservation wage), the worker has little income, so the income effect is small and the substitution effect dominates — higher wages lead to more hours worked. As wages continue to rise, the worker becomes wealthier and the income effect grows stronger. Eventually, the income effect may dominate, causing the supply curve to bend backward. The shape of this curve is one of the most important empirical questions in labour economics.

Your Turn 2

  • Suppose your wage was temporarily high for one week. How would this affect your labor supply?
    1. I would work more
    2. I would work the same amount
    3. I would work less
  • Suppose your wage was permanently higher. How would this affect your labor supply?
    1. I would work more
    2. I would work the same amount
    3. I would work less

A temporary wage increase has a negligible income effect — one week of higher pay barely changes your lifetime wealth. So the response is dominated by the substitution effect, and most people would work more. A permanent wage increase, however, makes you substantially wealthier over your lifetime, generating a large income effect that competes with the substitution effect. Many people would choose to work less (or the same) because the income effect of permanent wealth is strong. This distinction between temporary and permanent wage changes is central to the intertemporal substitution hypothesis discussed later in the chapter.

The Backward Bending Labor Supply Curve

The labour supply curve traces the relationship between the wage rate and hours of work. At wages below the reservation wage ($10 in this example), the person does not work. At wages greater than $10, the person enters the labour market. The upward-sloping segment of the labour supply curve implies that substitution effects are stronger initially — as the wage rises from $10 to $20, hours increase from 0 to 40. The backward-bending segment implies that income effects may dominate eventually — as the wage rises from $20 to $25, hours actually fall from 40 to 30.

The Backward Bending Labor Supply Curve — Derivation

The left panel shows the optimal consumption bundles at various wage rates. The reservation wage is $10, where the person is indifferent between working and not working. No labour is supplied for wages at or below $10. Above $10, the person works varying hours: 20 hours at $13, 40 hours at $20, and 30 hours at $25. The right panel maps these wage-hours pairs onto a standard labour supply diagram. Initially, the curve is positively sloped as the substitution effect dominates. Beyond $20, the income effect dominates, leading to declining hours as the wage rises further. This creates the characteristic backward-bending shape.

Labor Supply Elasticity

  • The labor supply elasticity (\(\sigma\)) measures responsiveness in hours worked to changes in the wage rate

\[\sigma = \frac{\text{Percent change in hours worked}}{\text{Percent change in wage rate}}\]

  • Labor supply elasticity less than 1 is inelastic — hours of work respond proportionally less than the change in wages

  • Labor supply elasticity greater than 1 is elastic — hours of work respond proportionally more than the change in wages

The labour supply elasticity is the key empirical parameter in this chapter. Decades of research have produced a rough consensus: for prime-age men, the elasticity is small and often slightly negative (around -0.1), consistent with a mildly backward-bending supply curve. For women, the elasticity is positive and larger (around +0.2 to +0.4), meaning women’s hours are more responsive to wage changes. These estimates have enormous policy implications — they determine how much labour supply responds to tax changes, welfare programs, and wage subsidies like the EITC.

Your Turn 4

Suppose an individual’s wage rate decreases and the income effect dominates the substitution effect.

  1. What will be the impact on the desired hours of work?
  2. What is the relevant segment of the person’s labor supply curve?

When a worker’s wage rate declines and the income effect dominates the substitution effect, the person will work more hours. The logic: a wage decrease makes the worker poorer (income effect pushes toward less leisure, i.e., more work) and also makes leisure cheaper (substitution effect pushes toward more leisure, i.e., less work). If the income effect dominates, the net result is more work. The relevant segment of the labor supply curve is the backward-bending portion — where wages and hours move in opposite directions.


Policy Applications


Welfare Programs and Work Incentives

  • Cash grants reduce wage incentives

  • Welfare programs create work disincentives (especially for low-wage earners)

  • Welfare reduces the supply of labor by increasing non-labour income, which raises the reservation wage

Welfare programs are the most direct policy application of the labour-leisure model. A cash transfer to non-workers raises non-labour income, shifting the budget line up and increasing the reservation wage. If the transfer is conditional on not working (a “take-it-or-leave-it” grant), it creates a stark discontinuity in the opportunity set. If earnings are taxed against the grant, both income and substitution effects push toward fewer hours worked. This analysis explains why means-tested welfare programs have historically been criticised for reducing labour supply among the poor.

Effect of a Cash Grant on Work Incentives

Opposition to welfare stems from the belief that it fosters dependency. Consider a take-it-or-leave-it cash grant of $1,000 per month for non-workers. If you enter the labour force, you lose the entire grant regardless of your earnings. Without the program, the budget line FE gives an interior solution at point P — the worker enjoys 70 hours of leisure and works 40 hours. With the $1,000 grant, point G is added to the opportunity set. The woman receives $1,000 if she does not work. If she enters the labour market, the grant is revoked and she returns to the original budget line FE. For some workers, G will lie on a higher indifference curve than P, and they will choose not to work.

Effect of a Welfare Program on Hours: $1,000 Cash, 50% Tax

This figure introduces a more realistic welfare program where benefits are reduced gradually rather than eliminated all at once. The program provides a $1,000 cash grant but imposes a 50% tax on earnings — for every dollar earned, the grant is reduced by 50 cents. Without welfare, the worker is at point P. The welfare program generates both income and substitution effects, both of which reduce work hours. The income effect arises from the cash grant itself (the shift from E to G as the endowment point), and the substitution effect arises from the implicit 50% tax rate, which halves the net wage.

Effect of a Welfare Program on Hours (cont.)

The welfare program shifts the endowment from E to G — a $1,000 non-work grant. The net wage drops from $10 to $5, reflected in the flatter slope of the new budget line HG. The cash grant combined with the earnings tax must decrease work hours unambiguously: the income effect moves the worker from P to Q (more leisure due to higher income), and the substitution effect moves from Q to R (even more leisure because the effective wage is halved by the 50% tax).

Effect of a Welfare Program on Hours (cont.)

The hypothetical budget line DD is drawn parallel to the pre-welfare budget line and tangent to the new indifference curve. This isolates the pure income effect — the move from P to Q — showing what would happen if only the cash grant were provided without any implicit tax. The remaining move from Q to R captures the pure substitution effect of the 50% earnings tax. Both effects reinforce each other in reducing hours of work, which is why this type of welfare program unambiguously discourages labour supply.

Effect of a Welfare Program on Hours (cont.)

The income effect increases leisure: Q lies to the right of P, showing that the cash grant alone causes the worker to consume more leisure. The substitution effect then moves the worker further right, from Q to R: the 50% earnings tax halves the price of leisure (in terms of forgone after-tax earnings), boosting leisure demand further. The combined effect is unambiguously negative for labour supply — the worker at R works fewer hours than at P.

Effect of a Welfare Program on Hours (cont.)

This final panel shows the complete decomposition. The income effect from the cash grant moves the worker from P to Q. The substitution effect from the 50% implicit tax rate moves the worker from Q to R. Both effects push in the same direction — toward more leisure and fewer hours of work. This is the fundamental insight: welfare programs that combine a cash grant with an earnings tax create doubly reinforced work disincentives. This analysis motivated the shift toward “making work pay” programs like the EITC.


The Earned Income Tax Credit


The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

  • Since 1975, a major financial boost for low-income working individuals (e.g., working mother with two qualifying children)

  • By 2015, the leading US cash-benefit program, disbursing nearly $70 billion

  • In 2017, EITC offers up to 40% tax credit on earnings, maxing at $5,616 for income up to $14,040

  • Eligible for this maximum credit as long as earnings are between $14,040 and $18,340

  • Credit phases out after $18,340, reducing by 21.06 cents for each additional dollar earned — fully phased out at $45,007

The EITC is the mirror image of the welfare programs just discussed. Instead of paying people not to work, it pays people to work. The credit has three phases: the subsidy phase (where each dollar earned is topped up by 40 cents), the plateau phase (where the credit stays at its maximum of $5,616), and the phase-out range (where the credit is reduced by 21.06 cents for each additional dollar earned). This design was deliberately chosen to encourage labour force participation while maintaining some redistribution. The EITC has become the largest anti-poverty program in the US, dwarfing traditional welfare.

EITC Calculation: Phase-In

  • For every dollar earned, she gets 40 cents as a tax credit

  • If she earns $10,000 → Credit = $4,000

  • If she earns $14,040 → Credit = $5,616 (maximum)

  • Earnings $14,040 to $18,340: receives the full maximum credit of $5,616

The phase-in region of the EITC effectively subsidises work at a 40% rate. A worker earning $10,000 receives $4,000 in credit, bringing her total income to $14,000. At $14,040 in earnings, the credit reaches its maximum of $5,616. Between $14,040 and $18,340, the credit remains flat at $5,616 regardless of earnings — this is the plateau region. In the phase-in region, the net wage is 1.4 times the gross wage, creating a powerful incentive to enter the labour force and work at least some hours.

The EITC and the Budget Line

Above $18,340, the EITC phases out at 21.06 cents per dollar. A worker earning $10 per hour at $18,340 who works an extra hour earns $10 gross but loses $2.11 in EITC, yielding a net wage of $7.89 per hour. This is represented by segment GH of the budget line, which is flatter than the original no-EITC budget line. At $45,007, the EITC is fully phased out and the budget line reverts to its original slope (segment FG). The figure assumes zero non-labour income.

The EITC and the Budget Line (cont.)

In the plateau phase ($14,040 to $18,340), the EITC does not change the net wage — it generates a pure income effect. The worker receives a flat $5,616 boost regardless of exactly how many hours she works within this range. This is represented by segment HJ of the budget line, which is parallel to the original budget line but shifted up by $5,616. Since the net wage is unchanged, there is no substitution effect — only an income effect, which (if leisure is normal) reduces hours of work.

The EITC and the Budget Line (cont.)

The complete EITC budget line has a distinctive kinked shape with three segments. In the phase-in region, the net wage exceeds the gross wage (the budget line is steeper), creating both income and substitution effects that encourage work. In the plateau, the net wage equals the gross wage but income is higher, creating only an income effect. In the phase-out, the net wage is below the gross wage, with an income effect encouraging less work and a substitution effect also encouraging less work. The key policy insight is that the EITC unambiguously encourages labour force participation (entering the labour force is always rewarded) even though its effect on hours for those already working is ambiguous.

How Does the EITC Affect Labor Supply?

  • The EITC should increase labor force participation of non-workers in targeted groups

  • The EITC encourages some non-workers to start working and never encourages a worker to quit working

  • The EITC produces an income effect for all recipients

  • Hours worked should change — the direction depends on which phase the worker is in

The EITC’s participation effect is its strongest and most unambiguous prediction. For someone currently not working, the EITC raises the return to the first hour of work by 40%, making it more likely that the market wage exceeds the reservation wage. Importantly, the EITC never makes it optimal to quit working entirely — at zero hours, you get zero credit, so there is always some incentive to work at least a little. Empirical evidence strongly confirms the participation prediction: Eissa and Liebman (1996) found that the 1986 EITC expansion increased single mothers’ labour force participation by 2.8 percentage points. The hours effect is more nuanced and depends on the worker’s position along the budget constraint.


Labor Supply over the Life Cycle


Labor Supply Over the Life Cycle

  • Wage rates change over the worker’s life (over the life cycle)

  • Wages are low when young

  • Wages rise with time and peak around age 50

  • Wages decline or remain stable after age 50

  • The changes in wages over the life cycle are “evolutionary” wage changes that alter the price of leisure

Until now, we have analysed the labour supply decision in a single-period framework. But workers make labour supply decisions continuously over their entire careers, and the wage they face changes predictably as they age. Young workers earn relatively little, wages rise as they accumulate experience and human capital, peak in middle age, and then plateau or decline. These predictable, career-long wage changes are “evolutionary” — they change the price of leisure at different life stages without necessarily changing the worker’s lifetime wealth. This distinction between evolutionary and unexpected wage changes is central to the intertemporal substitution hypothesis.

Theoretical Issues of Evolutionary Wages

  • A person will work more hours when wages are higher (that is, the substitution effect tends to dominate the income effect)

  • The profile of hours of work over the life cycle will have the same shape as the age-earnings profile

  • Inter-temporal substitution hypothesis: people substitute their time over the life cycle to take advantage of changes in the price of leisure

The intertemporal substitution hypothesis is an elegant prediction: since evolutionary wage changes do not alter lifetime wealth (they are predictable and already factored into the worker’s life plan), they generate only a substitution effect. There is no income effect because the worker is not surprised by the wage change. This means the substitution effect dominates, and workers should work more hours when wages are high and fewer hours when wages are low. The testable implication is that hours of work should track the age-earnings profile — rising when young, peaking at 50, and declining thereafter.

The Life Cycle Path of Wages and Hours

This figure shows the parallel paths of wages and hours over a typical worker’s career. The age-earnings profile rises rapidly when the worker is young, peaks around age 50, and then stabilises or declines. The intertemporal substitution hypothesis predicts that the hours-of-work profile should have the same hump shape — and broadly, it does. Workers in their prime earning years tend to work the longest hours. Younger workers work less (partly because of schooling and lower wages), and older workers reduce hours as wages plateau and retirement approaches. The current single-period model overlooks this continuous career-long decision-making. Workers are not simply choosing hours today — they are trading today’s leisure for tomorrow’s consumption across their entire working life.

Hours of Work Over the Life Cycle: Different Wage Paths

Joe’s wage consistently exceeds Jack’s at every age. Both work more when wages are high, as the intertemporal substitution hypothesis predicts. But whether Joe works more or fewer total hours than Jack is ambiguous. Joe faces a higher wage at every age (substitution effect: work more), but his higher lifetime income means he is wealthier (income effect: consume more leisure). If the substitution effect dominates, Joe works more hours than Jack at every age. If the income effect dominates, Joe may actually work fewer hours despite his higher wage. This cross-section comparison introduces income effects that are absent from the within-person evolutionary analysis — a crucial distinction for empirical work.

Labor Force Participation Rates Over the Life Cycle, 2017

This figure from the CPS shows labour force participation rates by age for men and women in 2017. The patterns are broadly consistent with the life cycle model: participation rises sharply in the early twenties (as people leave school), plateaus during prime working years (25-54), and declines after 55 as workers approach retirement. The gender gap in participation has narrowed considerably over time but remains visible, especially during childbearing years. The steep decline after 60 reflects both the availability of retirement benefits and the declining evolutionary wage for older workers.

Labor Supply Over the Business Cycle: Added Worker Effect

  • So-called “secondary” workers currently out of the labor market are affected by a recession because the main breadwinner becomes unemployed or faces a wage cut

  • A secondary worker may choose to enter the labor force during these bad times

  • The labor force participation rate of secondary workers (the added worker effect) is counter-cyclical

The added worker effect extends the labour supply model to household decisions during recessions. When the primary earner loses a job or takes a pay cut, the household’s non-labour income effectively falls. This reduction in income raises the secondary worker’s incentive to enter the labour force — the reservation wage drops because the household needs the income. The prediction is counter-cyclical: participation of secondary workers rises during downturns. Empirically, the added worker effect exists but is generally found to be small, likely because many secondary workers also face poor job prospects during recessions.

Labor Supply Over the Business Cycle: Discouraged Worker Effect

  • Unemployed workers find it very difficult to find jobs during a recession, so they give up searching

  • Discouraged workers exit the labor force during bad times

  • The labor force participation rate of discouraged workers is pro-cyclical

The discouraged worker effect works in the opposite direction to the added worker effect. During a recession, job prospects deteriorate and the expected wage from searching falls. As the perceived market wage drops below the reservation wage, some workers stop searching entirely and leave the labour force. They are not counted as unemployed because they are no longer actively looking for work. This means the official unemployment rate understates the true labour market slack during recessions. The discouraged worker effect is pro-cyclical — participation falls during downturns and rises during expansions.

Labor Supply Over the Business Cycle: Net Effect

  • The discouraged worker effect dominates the added worker effect, especially during recessions

  • The Labor Force Participation Rate is pro-cyclical

The empirical evidence clearly shows that the discouraged worker effect is larger than the added worker effect. During recessions, the overall labour force participation rate falls — more people exit the labour force than enter it. This has important implications for measuring the true state of the labour market. The unemployment rate, which only counts people actively searching for work, systematically understates slack during downturns because it misses the discouraged workers who have dropped out entirely. The employment-population ratio is often a better cyclical indicator precisely because it captures this margin.

Summary

  • An increase in the wage generates both an income and a substitution effect

  • The labor supply elasticity is on the order of -0.1 for men and +0.2 for women

  • Welfare programs create work disincentives because they provide cash grants to participants as well as tax those recipients who enter the labor market. In contrast, credits on earned income create work incentives and draw many non-workers into the labor force.

The three key takeaways from Chapter 2 are: first, every wage change generates competing income and substitution effects, and the net impact on hours worked is an empirical question. Second, the empirical evidence suggests that men’s labour supply is quite inelastic (slightly backward-bending), while women’s is more elastic and upward-sloping. Third, the design of transfer programs matters enormously — welfare programs that tax earnings create double disincentives (income and substitution effects both reduce work), while the EITC’s subsidy phase creates powerful participation incentives. These insights have directly shaped the evolution of US anti-poverty policy from welfare toward work-based programs.

What Is Labor Market Discrimination?

  • Discrimination occurs when the marketplace takes into account factors such as race, sex, caste, or appearance when making economic exchanges.

  • These factors are not related to productivity — yet they affect hiring, wages, and occupational sorting.

  • The central question: how much of the observed wage gap between groups reflects skill differences and how much reflects discrimination?

  • Wage gaps can arise even without prejudice — employers may use socioeconomic background to fill information gaps about productivity
  • Opening prompt: “If two equally productive workers exist, should they earn the same?” — most say yes; then ask “Do they?”
  • India: Thorat & Attewell (2007) document caste discrimination in hiring; gender wage gaps persist after controlling for education

Types of Discrimination

Discriminatory Behaviours (Oxford English Dictionary)

  • Ableism — discrimination in favour of able-bodied persons.
  • Ageism — prejudice or discrimination on the grounds of age, especially against the elderly.
  • Fattism — prejudice or discrimination against fat people.
  • Heterosexism — prejudice and antagonism by heterosexual persons toward homosexuals.
  • Lookism — prejudice or discrimination on the grounds of appearance.
  • Racism — hatred or intolerance of another race or other races.
  • Sexism — discrimination or devaluation based on a person’s sex, especially directed against women.
  • Key evidence by type:
    • Racism — Bertrand & Mullainathan (2004): white-sounding names get 50% more callbacks
    • Sexism — Goldin & Rouse (2000): blind auditions raised female hiring by 25–46%
    • Lookism — Hamermesh & Biddle (1994): 5–10% beauty premium, even in non-customer roles
    • Ableism — disabled workers in India earn 30–50% less after controls (World Bank 2007)
    • Ageism — older applicants get fewer callbacks despite equal qualifications (Neumark et al. 2019)
    • Fattism — BMI wage penalty is larger for women (Rudolph et al. 2009)
    • Heterosexism — gay men earned 5–10% less (Badgett 1995); India’s Section 377 struck down 2018
  • India: Thorat & Attewell (2007) replicate callback study for caste; “motherhood penalty” ~5% per child (Kleven et al. 2019)

Race and Gender in the Labor Market

  • Men earn more than women, and whites usually earn more than non-whites — these are raw wage differentials.

  • Differences in educational attainment between whites and non-whites account for a portion of the wage differential — but not all of it.

  • After controlling for education, experience, occupation, and region, a residual gap remains — this residual is often attributed to discrimination.

  • The key empirical challenge is decomposing the raw gap into an explained portion (skills) and an unexplained portion (discrimination).

  • Table 9-1: full-time white men earn ~$88.7K vs white women $65.7K — a ~35% raw gap
  • The unexplained residual is an upper bound on discrimination — may also reflect unmeasured skills, preferences, or selection
  • Wage gaps are not US-specific — similar race/gender patterns in Malaysia, Canada, Britain, Israel, India

What Is the Discrimination Coefficient?

  • Gary Becker (1957) formalised the notion of taste-based discrimination — racial prejudice translated into economic behaviour.

  • A prejudiced employer perceives the cost of hiring a Black worker as higher than the true cost.

  • Even though it costs \(w_B\) dollars to hire one person-hour of Black labour, the employer acts as if it costs \(w_B(1 + d)\), where \(d > 0\) is the discrimination coefficient.

  • A larger \(d\) means more prejudice: the employer requires a bigger wage discount before willingly hiring Black workers.

  • The framework extends to other agents:
    • Employees act as if wage is w_W(1 − d) alongside minorities
    • Customers act as if price is p(1 + d) when served by minorities
  • The coefficient “monetises” prejudice — translates a taste into a dollar cost
  • Key prediction: discrimination is costly to the employer — prejudice is an economic luxury
  • India: Banerjee et al. (2009) show caste-based hiring preferences operate as Becker-type taste discrimination

How Does a Non-Discriminating Firm Hire?

Figure 9-1: The Hiring Decision of a Firm That Does Not Discriminate

  • Reading the figure:
    • Horizontal red line = market Black wage w_B
    • Downward blue curve = VMP_E (value of marginal product)
    • Firm hires Black workers rightward until w_B = VMP_E at point **E*_B**
  • Key logic: both groups equally productive, but w_B < w_W → non-discriminator hires only Black workers (100% minority workforce)
  • Production function: q = f(E_W + E_B) — output depends on total workers, not type; MP is identical regardless of race

How Does a Prejudiced Firm Hire?

Figure 9-2: The Hiring Decision of a Prejudiced Firm

  • Decision rule (Eq. 9-3): compare w_W with w_B(1 + d) — if adjusted minority cost > w_W → hire only whites
  • Panel (a) — White Firm:
    • w_B(1 + d) > w_W → hires only white workers at w_W
    • Employment where w_W = VMP_E → point **E*_W**
  • Panel (b) — Black Firm shows three levels:
    • w_B(1 + d_1) highest → fewest workers at E¹_B
    • w_B(1 + d_0) middle → more workers at E⁰_B
    • w_B (no prejudice) → maximum employment at **E*_B**
  • Takeaway: as d falls from d_1 → d_0 → 0, employment rises — discrimination always means too few workers hired

Employer Discrimination: Key Implications

  • If Black and white workers are perfect substitutes, the Becker model predicts segregated workforces: each firm hires only one race.

  • Even non-discriminating employers end up with a segregated (all-Black) workforce — they hire the cheaper, equally productive group.

  • Discrimination does not pay: prejudiced employers hire the wrong type of worker and/or the wrong number of workers — reducing their profits.

  • In competitive markets, discriminating firms earn lower profits and should eventually be driven out by non-discriminating competitors.

  • Prejudiced firms pay for the right to discriminate out of their own pocket — wrong type and/or wrong number of workers
  • The most profitable firm has d = 0; discriminators earn lower profits → competitive pressure to exit
  • Segregation is endogenous: taste discrimination produces workplace segregation even without segregation laws
  • The puzzle: if discrimination is costly, why does it persist? → monopoly power, customer/employee discrimination, collusion, or coordination failure

Profits and Discrimination

Figure 9-3: Profits and the Discrimination Coefficient

  • Reading left to right:
    • At d = 0 → firm earns maximum profit π_max (non-discriminator)
    • As d increases → profit declines (line slopes down)
  • Left region (d < d_W) — “Black Firms”: still hire Black workers but at increasing perceived cost; profits fall
  • Threshold d_W (dotted vertical line): firm switches from hiring Black to hiring only white workers
  • Right region (d > d_W) — “White Firms”: all hire only whites at w_W; earn the same flat low profit π_W
  • Key gap: distance between π_max and π_W = the cost of discrimination — paid out of employer’s own pocket

Determination of the Relative Minority Wage

Figure 9-4: Determination of the Relative Minority Wage in the Labor Market

  • Curves:
    • Vertical red line S at N = fixed supply of Black workers
    • Downward blue line D = demand, starting from point R on y-axis
  • Reading top to bottom:
    • When ratio > 1, no firm hires minorities
    • As ratio falls along D, more firms are “compensated” for disutility → demand rises
    • At very low ratios, even highly prejudiced firms are “bought off”
  • Equilibrium: S and D intersect at (w_B/w_W)*, set by the marginal discriminator — always below 1 when all firms are prejudiced
  • Adjustment: above equilibrium → excess supply → wage falls; below → excess demand → wage rises

When Some Firms Have Negative Discrimination Coefficients

Figure 9-5: The Relative Minority Wage When Some Firms Have Negative d

  • Key shift: some firms prefer minorities (negative d) → they bid up minority wages
  • Demand curve D’ is shifted right/up compared to D in Fig 9-4
  • With sufficiently small minority supply N, equilibrium (w_B/w_W)* can exceed 1 — minorities earn more than majority workers
  • Implication: the observed wage gap measures the marginal firm’s d, not the average prejudice level — distribution matters more than the mean
  • The wage disadvantage should not be interpreted as a summary of total prejudice in the market

Employee Discrimination

  • Employee discrimination: white workers dislike working alongside Black workers — they require a compensating differential (higher wage) to do so.

  • Key result: employee discrimination does not generate a wage differential between equally skilled Black and white workers.

  • Employee discrimination does not affect the profitability of firms — the cost is borne by prejudiced workers themselves (they sort into segregated workplaces).

  • The outcome is workplace segregation: Black and white workers end up in different firms, but at the same wage.

  • Mechanism: white workers act as if wage is w_W(1 − d) in an integrated workplace — disutility from working alongside minorities
  • A profit-maximising employer never chooses integration — “it does not pay to mix”; cheaper to segregate entirely
  • Competition for cheapest workers equalises wages across segregated firms — both groups earn their VMP, just in separate firms
  • Key contrast: unlike employer discrimination, no market forces diminish employee discrimination over time — no profit motive to eliminate it

Customer Discrimination

  • If customers discriminate, their perceived price of a good is utility-adjusted with a discrimination coefficient: they act as if the price is \(p(1 + d_c)\) when served by a Black worker.

  • When a firm cannot hide Black workers from customers, customer discrimination can have an adverse effect on Black wages.

  • Customer discrimination is most relevant in customer-facing occupations (sales, services, professional consultations).

  • Firms may respond by assigning minority workers to back-office roles — creating occupational segregation even within firms.

  • When it bites: harms minority wages only in “contact” (customer-facing) jobs where firms cannot hide workers
  • If firms can allocate workers → minorities placed in non-contact roles → customer discrimination may not matter much
  • Evidence:
    • Table 9-2: contact vs. non-contact comparison → DiD estimate = 14.6 percentage points
    • Baseball cards: white players’ cards cost 10–13% more than Black players’, controlling for stats
  • Why it persists: firms cannot force customers to be unprejudiced; competitive forces cannot easily eliminate it
  • India: customer discrimination may operate through caste in food service, domestic work, and personal services

What Is Statistical Discrimination?

  • Statistical discrimination occurs when employers base decisions on the average characteristics of the group to which a worker belongs.

  • Employers use imperfect predictors of productivity: age, education, and experience provide some information — but not enough.

  • Employers may use race and gender as well, since these also provide statistical information about group averages.

  • Example: young males pay higher car insurance rates because, on average, they have more accidents — even though many individual young men are safe drivers.

  • Key distinction: wage differences arise even without prejudice — group membership carries information
  • No malice required: a “colour-blind, gender-blind, profit-maximising employer” still discriminates if group averages differ
  • Applicants from high-productivity groups benefit; those from low-productivity groups are penalised regardless of individual merit
  • Formalised by Phelps (1972) and Arrow (1973) — rational but unfair use of group-level information

Statistical Discrimination: The Mechanism

  • Gender may provide information on job commitment since women on average have higher turnover rates.

  • Race may provide information about schooling quality since Black workers on average attended lower-quality schools than white workers.

  • The employer is not being malicious — they are using all available information to minimise hiring costs.

  • The problem: workers whose individual characteristics differ from the group average are harmed — a high-commitment woman or a well-educated Black worker is judged by the group mean, not their own traits.

  • Core problem: employers cannot perfectly observe individual productivity → use group membership as a noisy signal (adverse selection)
  • Efficient but unfair: minimises hiring costs for the firm, but harms individuals who deviate from their group average
  • Insurance analogy: firms routinely use group info — life insurance by gender, auto insurance by age; “prior experience about group averages will surely matter”
  • Convergence prediction: statistical discrimination diminishes as group averages converge — male-female turnover rates are indeed converging

Statistical Discrimination: Implications

  • Employers are not harmed when they practise statistical discrimination — they gain since they minimise hiring costs.

  • Statistical discrimination will diminish if the average characteristics of the groups converge over time.

  • Male-female turnover rates are converging — this should reduce gender-based statistical discrimination.

  • Policy challenge: banning the use of group characteristics (e.g., race or gender) in hiring forces employers to use less informative signals — but it protects individuals from being judged by group averages.

  • Surprise: statistical discrimination does not necessarily predict average Blacks earn less — it benefits some while harming others
  • Self-fulfilling prophecy: if employers invest less in women’s training (expecting high turnover), women rationally have higher turnover → discrimination becomes self-reinforcing (Coate & Loury 1993)
  • Policy: banning group characteristics (Title VII, EEO) forces employers to use less informative signals — justified even when statistical discrimination is “rational”
  • India: reservation policies address both taste and statistical discrimination; caste-based hiring stereotypes may function as statistical discrimination (Deshpande 2011)

Non-Discrimination Factors

  • Raw racial and gender differentials in earnings, occupational distribution, and unemployment must be interpreted with caution.

  • Factors other than discrimination may play a role:

  • Individual choice: women may choose occupations with greater flexibility for family responsibilities.

  • Pre-market human capital: differences in schooling quality, field of study, and work experience.

  • The challenge is separating constrained choices (driven by discrimination or social norms) from free choices (driven by genuine preferences).

  • Same years of schooling ≠ same skills: groups “attend institutions that vary in quality” → Oaxaca produces a biased measure
  • Omitted variable problem: “anyone who doubts discrimination can always point out a variable was left out” — the residual is never definitive
  • Pre-market discrimination: “cultural discrimination as well as differential funding” of schools influences human capital before labour market entry
  • “Choices” are themselves shaped by social norms, stereotypes, and discrimination (Blau & Kahn 2017) — separating constrained from free choices is the core challenge

How Do We Measure Discrimination?

  • One possible measure is the difference in mean wages — but this confounds discrimination with skill differences.

  • A better measure would compare the wages of equally skilled workers from different groups.

  • The Oaxaca decomposition (1973): a technique that decomposes the raw wage differential into:

  • A portion related to differences in skills (explained component).

  • A portion attributable to labour market discrimination (unexplained component).

  • Oaxaca-Blinder (1973): the standard tool for splitting a wage gap into explained (skills) and unexplained (discrimination) components
  • Raw gap Δw = w̄_M − w̄_F is “unappealing — comparing apples and oranges”; need to adjust for skill differences
  • Method: estimate separate wage regressions for each group → decompose into discrimination (different returns to same characteristics) and skills (different characteristics at male returns)
  • Limitation: the unexplained component is an upper bound on discrimination — may include unmeasured productivity differences
  • India: Madheswaran & Attewell (2007) apply Oaxaca to caste wage gaps (NSS data); find significant unexplained residuals for SC/ST workers

The Oaxaca–Blinder Decomposition

  • Raw wage gap: \(\Delta w = \color{#0000DC}{\bar{w}_M} - \color{#F01928}{\bar{w}_F}\) — “comparing apples and oranges” (confounds skills with discrimination).

  • Estimate separate earnings functions for each group: \[\color{#0000DC}{w_M = \alpha_M + \beta_M\, s_M} \qquad \color{#F01928}{w_F = \alpha_F + \beta_F\, s_F}\]

  • No discrimination: \(\alpha_M = \alpha_F\) and \(\beta_M = \beta_F\) — employers value skills equally.

  • Decomposition (Eq. 9-8): \[\Delta w = \underbrace{\left\{(\alpha_M - \alpha_F) + (\beta_M - \beta_F)\bar{s}_F\right\}}_{\color{#F01928}{\text{Due to discrimination}}} + \underbrace{\beta_M(\bar{s}_M - \bar{s}_F)}_{\color{#0000DC}{\text{Due to skills}}}\]

  • The trick (Eq. 9-8): add and subtract β_M × s̄_F → splits the raw gap into two clean components
  • Discrimination component = (α_M − α_F) + (β_M − β_F)s̄_F — the gap if women had the same schooling as men
  • Skills component = β_M(s̄_M − s̄_F) — the gap because men have more schooling; zero if s̄_M = s̄_F
  • Caveat: “look carefully at the fine print” — the discrimination share changes dramatically with control variables (Table 9-3)

Statistical Discrimination and Wages

  • The firm’s wage offer is a weighted average of the applicant’s own score \(\color{#0000DC}{T}\) and the group average \(\color{#F01928}{\bar{T}}\): \[w = (1 - \alpha)\,\color{#F01928}{\bar{T}} + \alpha\,\color{#0000DC}{T}\]

  • \(\alpha = 1\): test perfectly predicts — wage depends only on own score; \(\alpha = 0\): own score meaningless — employer relies entirely on group average.

  • \(\alpha\) = predictive power of the screening test — higher \(\alpha\) means less reliance on group membership.

  • Statistical discrimination affects both the intercept and the slope of the wage–test-score line.

  • T = numerical score combining all screening info (résumé, interview, tests); if perfectly correlated, a score of 15 → VMP of $15
  • Realism: perfect prediction is unrealistic — “some low-scoring applicants will turn out to be quite productive, and some high-scoring applicants will be spectacular failures”
  • Two channels through which statistical discrimination affects wages:
    1. Intercept — different group averages T̄ shift the wage line up/down
    2. Slope — different α values tilt the line (Fig 9-6 illustrates both)
  • Even a “colour-blind, gender-blind, profit-maximising employer” will use group membership when individual signals are noisy

Determinants of the White–Black Wage Ratio

  • There has been an upward trend in the wages of Black workers relative to white workers in recent decades.

  • This has been attributed to increases in the quality and quantity of Black schooling.

  • Government programs — civil rights legislation, affirmative action, and equal employment opportunity enforcement — have positively affected Black wages.

  • However, the convergence has stalled since the mid-1970s, and the Black-white wage ratio has remained roughly constant for decades.

  • Table 9-3 (Oaxaca):
    • Raw gap = −0.211
    • With education/age/region → skills = −0.082, discrimination = −0.134
    • Adding occupation/industry → skills = −0.114, discrimination = −0.098
  • Fine print warning: discrimination share changes dramatically with control variables — “look carefully at the fine print”
  • Rising returns to skill post-1980 may have offset anti-discrimination gains
  • Schooling convergence: in 1940, white men had 9.9 years vs 6.0 for Black; by 1980 gap was only 1.4 years; quality gap also narrowed

Discrimination Against Other Groups

  • Differences in wages across ethnic groups can be linked to varying educational attainment.

  • Less skilled workers earn less, consistent with human capital theory.

  • Asian workers tend to earn more than white workers, mainly due to higher levels of schooling.

  • The Hispanic-white wage gap has remained relatively stable, reflecting persistent differences in educational attainment and immigration patterns.

  • Hispanic gap: over three-quarters attributable to differences in educational attainment — especially among Mexican-origin workers
  • Asian advantage: Asian men earned ~30% more than white men in 2020; driven by high educational attainment
  • Demographic shifts: Hispanic population grew 6.4% (1980) → 18.5% (2019); Asian from 1.5% → 5.9%
  • “Shades of Black”: light skin = $14.42/hr, medium = $13.23, dark = $11.72 (vs $15.94 white men); light-skinned immigrants earn 17% more than dark-skinned
  • Speech discrimination: Black workers whose speech is “distinctly identified” earn about 12% less than those who sound “white”

Determinants of the Male–Female Wage Ratio

  • Occupational crowding has segregated women into particular occupations where the return to education is lower.

  • Human capital is more profitable the longer the payoff period — women who anticipated career interruptions historically invested in occupations with lower depreciation rates.

  • Women are better off if they enter occupations in which their skills do not deteriorate during years spent in the household sector.

  • The gender wage gap has narrowed substantially since 1980, driven by rising female education, delayed fertility, and changing occupational choices.

  • Occupational crowding evidence: fewer than 6% of aircraft mechanics are women, but 98%+ of kindergarten teachers; a woman in a 75%+ female occupation earns 14% less than one in a 75%+ male occupation
  • Table 9-4 (Oaxaca):
    • Raw gap = −0.286
    • With education/age/region → skills = −0.008, discrimination = −0.279
    • Adding occupation/industry → skills = −0.076, discrimination = −0.211
  • Key omitted variable: same-age men and women may have very different work experience
  • Case studies:
    • Michigan Law: 15 years post-graduation, men $141K vs women $86K — two-thirds due to work history
    • Booth MBA: no gap at start, but men earn 30% more after 5 years, 60% after 15; 40%+ of women had career interruptions vs 10% of men
    • Uber: no algorithm bias, yet men earn 7% more — explained by profitable hours, more experience, driving 4% faster
  • Goldin (2014): the “last chapter” of convergence requires workplace flexibility; gap largest where long hours are disproportionately rewarded
  • India: female LFP declining (U-shape hypothesis); gaps persist even in formal sector

Review — Introduction

  • Discrimination occurs when the marketplace accounts for factors (race, sex, caste) unrelated to productivity.
  • Raw wage differentials between groups partly reflect skill differences and partly reflect discrimination.
  • The empirical challenge is decomposing the gap into explained (skills) and unexplained (discrimination) components.

Review — Taste Discrimination (Becker)

  • The discrimination coefficient \(d\) makes the employer perceive minority labour as costing \(w_B(1+d)\) rather than \(w_B\).
  • With perfect substitutes, the Becker model predicts segregated workforces — each firm hires only one race.
  • Discrimination is costly: prejudiced firms earn lower profits and should be driven out by competitive forces.
  • Persistence of discrimination suggests imperfect competition or non-employer sources of prejudice.

Review — Employee, Customer & Statistical Discrimination

  • Employee discrimination produces workplace segregation but not wage gaps.
  • Customer discrimination creates wage gaps in customer-facing occupations; competitive forces cannot easily eliminate it.
  • Statistical discrimination is rational but unfair: employers judge individuals by group averages when individual signals are noisy.
  • Statistical discrimination diminishes as group characteristics converge.

Review — Measuring Discrimination & Policy Evidence

  • The Oaxaca decomposition splits the raw wage gap into explained (skill differences) and unexplained (discrimination) components.
  • The Black-white wage ratio rose sharply after the Civil Rights Act but has stalled since the mid-1970s.
  • Declining Black male LFP biases the measured wage ratio upward (selection effect).
  • The gender wage gap has narrowed from ~60% to ~82% since 1980, driven by rising female education and changing occupational choices.

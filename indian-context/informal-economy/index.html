    India's Informal Economy¶

    If you had to choose a single fact to characterise the Indian labour market, it would be this: the overwhelming majority of workers are informal. They work without written contracts, without social security, and without the legal protections that labour law nominally provides. Understanding informality is not a niche topic in Indian labour economics -- it is the central topic.

    Defining Informality¶

    Informality is a concept with multiple, overlapping definitions. The two most influential for India are the enterprise-based definition (informal sector) and the job-based definition (informal employment).

    The NCEUS Framework¶

    The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector (NCEUS, 2007, 2009), chaired by Arjun Sengupta, provided the most widely cited definitions for the Indian context:

    NCEUS Definitions

    • Informal sector (unorganised sector): All unincorporated private enterprises owned by individuals or households engaged in the sale or production of goods and services, operated on a proprietary or partnership basis, with fewer than 10 workers (or fewer than 20 if not using power).

    • Informal employment: Workers who do not receive employment-related social security benefits (provident fund, pension, gratuity, health care) from their employer, regardless of whether the enterprise is formal or informal.

    The critical insight of the NCEUS framework is that these two dimensions are distinct. A worker can be in the formal sector but hold an informal job -- think of a contract cleaner working inside a large corporation without any benefits. The NCEUS estimated that in 2004-05, roughly 86 per cent of total employment was informal employment, while about 93 per cent of workers were in the informal sector. These numbers have changed only modestly since then.

    ILO Framework¶

    The International Labour Organization uses a similar two-dimensional framework. The 15th International Conference of Labour Statisticians (ICLS, 1993) defined the informal sector based on enterprise characteristics, while the 17th ICLS (2003) introduced the concept of informal employment based on the nature of the job. India's NCEUS definitions are broadly aligned with the ILO framework, though the employment-size thresholds differ from those used in other countries.

    Size and Structure¶

    The numbers are staggering:

    Indicator Estimate
    Informal sector employment (% of total) ~89% (PLFS 2023-24)
    Informal employment (% of total non-agricultural) ~75-80%
    Contribution to GDP ~50% (NCEUS estimate)
    Self-employment as share of total ~55-57%
    Casual labour as share of total ~22-24%

    The Composition of Informality

    The informal workforce is not homogeneous. It spans a wide spectrum:

    • Own-account workers (street vendors, small farmers, artisans) -- the largest group.
    • Contributing family workers (unpaid work on family farms or enterprises) -- disproportionately women.
    • Casual wage labourers (daily-wage construction workers, agricultural labourers) -- the most precarious group.
    • Regular informal workers (contract workers in factories, domestic workers) -- growing as formal firms outsource.

    Each of these groups faces different constraints and responds differently to policy. Treating "the informal sector" as a monolith obscures more than it reveals.

    Characteristics of Informal Work¶

    Informal employment in India is characterised by several features that distinguish it sharply from the textbook model of competitive labour markets:

    Low and volatile earnings. Informal workers earn, on average, a fraction of what formal workers with similar observable characteristics earn. The NCEUS (2007) estimated that the average daily earnings of informal workers were roughly one-fourth those of formal workers. Earnings are also highly variable -- seasonal, weather-dependent, and subject to demand shocks.

    Absence of social protection. The defining feature of informal employment is the lack of employer-provided social security. No provident fund, no health insurance, no pension, no paid leave, no gratuity. This means informal workers face old age, illness, and income shocks with almost no buffer.

    Poor working conditions. Occupational health and safety standards are rarely observed. Construction workers, waste pickers, brick kiln labourers, and garment workers routinely face hazardous conditions without protective equipment or compensation for injuries. The compensating differentials framework (Chapter 5 of Borjas) predicts that such jobs should command a wage premium -- but in the Indian informal sector, the opposite often holds, because workers lack outside options.

    Why Compensating Differentials Fail in the Informal Sector

    The hedonic wage model assumes workers can freely choose between jobs with different risk-wage bundles. But if workers are constrained by caste, location, lack of education, or sheer desperation, they cannot "sort" into their preferred risk-wage combination. Instead, the worst jobs -- dangerous, dirty, and degrading -- are filled by the most disadvantaged workers at the lowest wages. This is the anti-compensating differential: bad jobs with bad pay, a pattern that the standard model cannot explain without incorporating labour market segmentation.

    Informal-Formal Linkages¶

    The informal and formal sectors are not separate economies. They are deeply interlinked through subcontracting chains, labour supply relationships, and product market competition.

    • Subcontracting: Large formal-sector firms routinely outsource production steps to small informal enterprises. The garment industry is a classic example: a branded export house (formal) contracts cutting and stitching to workshops (informal) that employ casual workers at piece rates. The formal firm captures the value; the informal workers bear the risk.

    • Disguised wage employment: Many nominally "self-employed" workers in India are, in economic substance, dependent employees. Auto-rickshaw drivers who lease vehicles from fleet owners, delivery workers for platform companies, and home-based garment workers producing for a single contractor all fall into this grey zone.

    • Labour supply: The informal sector serves as a vast reservoir of labour for the formal sector during demand peaks, and absorbs workers ejected during downturns. This is not a smooth competitive adjustment -- it is a segmented market with large transition costs.

    Theoretical Frameworks¶

    Lewis Dual-Economy Model¶

    Arthur Lewis (1954) proposed that developing economies have a traditional sector with unlimited supplies of labour at a subsistence wage, and a modern sector that draws workers from this pool as it accumulates capital. Growth occurs through the expansion of the modern sector, and the turning point arrives when the surplus labour in the traditional sector is exhausted and wages begin to rise.

    Lewis Applied to India

    India's informal sector can be thought of as Lewis's traditional sector -- a vast pool of underemployed workers available at low wages. The question is whether India has reached the Lewis turning point. Some scholars (e.g., Himanshu, 2011) have argued that rising real wages in rural India during 2007-2012 suggested an approaching turning point, though the subsequent slowdown in wage growth and persistent surplus labour cast doubt on this interpretation.

    Harris-Todaro Model¶

    Harris and Todaro (1970) extended the Lewis framework by explaining why rural-urban migration continues even when urban unemployment is high. In their model, migrants compare the expected urban wage (formal sector wage multiplied by the probability of getting a formal job) with the certain rural wage. Equilibrium can feature persistent urban unemployment and an informal urban sector where workers queue for formal jobs.

    The Harris-Todaro model maps well onto Indian cities. The sprawling urban informal sectors of Mumbai, Delhi, and Bangalore can be interpreted as waiting pools of workers hoping to transition into formal employment. Whether this "queue" interpretation or a "segmented markets" interpretation is correct remains debated.

    Segmented Labour Market Theory¶

    A third perspective, associated with Doeringer and Piore (1971) and extended to developing countries by Fields (1975, 2005), argues that formal and informal labour markets are structurally segmented, not merely differentiated by worker productivity. Institutional barriers -- labour regulations, unionisation, minimum wages, hiring networks -- create rents in the formal sector that are not competed away. Informal workers are not merely less productive; they are rationed out of formal employment.

    Evidence on Segmentation in India

    Ramaswamy (2012, Indian Journal of Labour Economics) and Nath (2020) find evidence of significant formal-informal wage gaps in India that persist after controlling for education, experience, industry, and occupation. The residual gap -- often 30-60 per cent -- is consistent with segmentation rather than pure human capital differences. However, the direction of causality is difficult to establish, and unobserved heterogeneity remains a concern.

    Recent Trends: Has Formalisation Happened?¶

    The question of whether India's economy is formalising has been hotly debated, particularly since 2016. Several policy interventions have been cited as potential drivers of formalisation:

    • Demonetisation (November 2016): The sudden withdrawal of high-denomination currency notes was partly justified as a blow against the "black economy," though its impact on actual formalisation is debated.

    • Goods and Services Tax (July 2017): By creating a unified indirect tax with an input tax credit chain, GST incentivised firms to register and transact formally. Early evidence suggests an increase in the number of GST-registered firms, though this does not automatically translate into formal employment.

    • Digital payments and UPI: The explosion of digital transactions has increased the traceability of economic activity, potentially nudging enterprises toward formal status.

    • EPFO enrolment data: The government has pointed to rising enrolments in the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) as evidence of formalisation. However, EPFO data conflate new formal jobs with formalisation of existing jobs and with multiple registrations by the same worker.

    The Formalisation Debate

    Abraham and co-authors (2023, Indian Journal of Labour Economics) argue that much of the apparent formalisation reflects a shift from self-employment to low-quality regular wage work -- "formalisation without security." Workers gain a regular employer but not social protection. Meanwhile, Ravi and co-authors (2019) suggest that firm registration has increased but the quality of formal jobs has not improved commensurately. The bottom line: formalisation in India is a process that is underway but remains shallow and incomplete.

    Policy Responses¶

    India has pursued several policy strategies to address the vulnerabilities of informal workers:

    e-Shram Portal (2021): A national database of unorganised workers, intended to serve as a single point of access to social welfare schemes. By early 2024, over 290 million workers had registered. The challenge is translating registration into actual benefit delivery.

    PM-SYM (Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan-dhan, 2019): A voluntary pension scheme for unorganised workers earning below Rs. 15,000 per month, providing Rs. 3,000 per month after age 60. Take-up has been lower than hoped -- fewer than 5 million enrollees by 2024 -- reflecting the classic problem of getting informal workers to commit to regular monthly contributions.

    Code on Social Security, 2020: One of the four new labour codes that consolidate 29 existing laws. The Code extends social security provisions to gig workers, platform workers, and unorganised workers. However, the rules under the Code have not yet been fully notified by most states as of early 2026, and implementation remains uncertain.

    PM Street Vendor's AtmaNirbhar Nidhi (PM SVANidhi, 2020): A micro-credit scheme for street vendors launched during COVID-19, providing working capital loans of up to Rs. 50,000 without collateral.

    The Policy Trilemma

    Formalising the informal sector faces a trilemma: (1) extending legal protections raises compliance costs for small firms, potentially destroying the very jobs it seeks to improve; (2) providing social security to workers outside formal employment relationships requires a financing mechanism that does not exist; and (3) registration and documentation requirements exclude the most vulnerable workers, who lack the literacy and digital access to navigate bureaucratic processes. Policy design must navigate all three horns simultaneously.
